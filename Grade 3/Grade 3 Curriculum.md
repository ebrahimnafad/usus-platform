## 1. Tune Up

### 1. Place Value to 100 - Model Expanded Form
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.1, 2.NBT.3
- **Prerequisites:** 2-2
- **Description:** Visually represent the expanded form for 2-digit numbers. Use a progression of concrete and abstract models, including ten frames, Base 10 blocks and discs, and number bonds. Use both formal names and nicknames for each number. What's the nickname for 60? Since it has 6 tens and 0 ones, it is "six ten zero."

### 2. Place Value to 100 - Shift 10
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-5
- **Description:** Prepare for subtraction and division with regrouping. Use Base 10 discs and number bonds to model expanded form. What is 62? 62 = 60 + 2 = 6 tens + 2 ones. Shift 10 to make a new number bond – a "Funny Number." 62 is also 50 + 12 = 5 tens + 12 ones. Progress from 2 steps to shifting 10 in 1 step.

### 3. Add to 100 - Shift 10
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 2-26 ○ 2-25
- **Description:** Use number bonds and a partial sums strategy to add 2-digit numbers with regrouping. What's 36+15? Since 30+10 = 40, 6+5 = 11, and 40+11 = 51, 36+15 = 51. Find and add partial sums to get the total sum. Progress from number bonds to equations. With practice, use mental math to become fluent within 100.

### 4. Subtract to 100 - Shift 10
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 2-35 ○ 2-34
- **Description:** Use a partial differences strategy to subtract 2-digit numbers with regrouping. What's 71-15? There are not enough ones in 71, so shift 10. Think of 71 as 60 and 11. Since 60-10 = 50, 11-5 = 6, and 50+6 = 56, 71-15 = 56. Find and add partial differences to get the total difference. Progress from 2 number bonds to 1.

### 5. Add-To Word Problems to 100
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-37 ○ 2-13
- **Description:** Given the starting amount and the total, find the "change" – how many are added. Given the change and the total, find the starting amount. Use a part-total diagram to visually show the relationship between the parts and total. Solve a subtraction or addition equation to find the unknown "change" or "start".

### 6. Take-From Word Problems to 100
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-41
- **Description:** Given the starting quantity – the total – and how many are left, find the "change" – how many are taken away. Given the change and how many are left, find the total. Use a diagram to show the relationship between the parts and total. Solve a subtraction or addition equation to find the unknown "change" or "start."

### 7. Difference-Unknown Word Problems to 100
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-45, 2-44
- **Description:** Use bar models to find the difference between 2 quantities. Lisa read 52 books. David read 17 books. How many more books did Lisa read than David? How many fewer books did David read than Lisa? Make sense of the keywords "greater," "less," "more," and "fewer." Use addition or subtraction to find the difference.

### 8. Larger-Unknown Word Problems to 100
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-47, 2-46
- **Description:** Use bar models to visualize the relationship between 2 quantities and their difference. Given a smaller quantity and the difference, find the larger quantity. Understand context to make sense of keywords. Solve intuitive problems using "more" and non-intuitive problems using "less" and "fewer."

### 9. Smaller-Unknown Word Problems to 100
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-49, 2-48
- **Description:** Generalize from story problems where the larger quantity is unknown, to comparison problems where the smaller quantity is unknown. Understand context to make sense of keywords. Solve intuitive problems using "less" and "fewer" to describe the difference, and non-intuitive problems using "more."

### 10. Place Value to 1,000 - Shift 100 or 10
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.1, 2.NBT.3
- **Prerequisites:** 2-80, 2-79
- **Description:** Prepare for subtraction with regrouping by modeling expanded form with Base 10 discs. What's 458? It is 4 hundreds, 5 tens, and 8 ones, or 400+50+8. If 100 is shifted to the tens, 458 becomes 300+150+8. If 10 is shifted instead of 100, 458 becomes 400+40+18. Start with Base 10 discs. Progress to using number bonds.

### 11. Place Value to 1,000 - Shift 100 & 10
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.1, 2.NBT.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-10 ○ 2-82
- **Description:** Prepare for subtraction with 2 regroupings by shifting both 100 and 10. What's 549? Its expanded form is 5 hundreds, 4 tens, and 9 ones, or 500+40+9. Shift 100 to make 400+140+9. Shift 10 to make 400+130+19. Progress from Base 10 discs to using number bonds. Start by writing 3 number bonds. Goal is to write just 1 number bond.

### 12. Add to 1,000 with 1 Regrouping
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.7, 3.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-94, 2-93
- **Description:** Add 3-digit numbers with 1 regrouping. What's 382+145? Use Base 10 discs to model expanded form. Find and add partial sums to get the total sum. 300+100 = 400, 80+40 = 120, and 2+5 = 7. Shift 100 from 120 to 400. Since 500+20+7 = 527, 382+145 = 527. Start with Base 10 discs and progress to using number bonds.

### 13. Add to 1,000 with 2 Regroupings
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.7, 3.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-12 ○ 2-95
- **Description:** Transition to adding with 2 regroupings. What's 245+186? Model expanded form with Base 10 discs. Find and add partial sums to get the total sum. 200+100 = 300, 40+80 = 120, and 5+6 = 11. Shift 100 from the 120, and 10 from the 11. Since 400+30+1 = 431, 245+186 = 431. Use number bonds when ready.

### 14. Subtract to 1,000 with 1 Regrouping
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.7, 3.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** 2-105, 2-104
- **Description:** Use Base 10 discs and number bonds to subtract by regrouping once. What's 675-392? Compare hundreds, tens, and ones. Need more tens, so shift 100. Think of 675 as 500, 170, and 5. Since 500-300 = 200, 170-90 = 80, and 5-2 = 3, 675-392 = 283. Add the partial differences to get the total difference.

### 15. Subtract to 1,000 with 2 Regroupings
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.7, 3.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-13 ○ 2-106
- **Description:** Use Base 10 discs and number bonds to subtract by regrouping twice. What's 532-158? Compare hundreds, tens, and ones. There are not enough tens, so shift 100. There are also not enough ones, so shift 10. Think of 532 as 400, 120, and 12. Since 400-100 = 300, 120-50 = 70, 12-8 = 4, and 300+70+4 = 374, 532-158 = 374.

## 2. Numbers to 1,000

### 16. 1-Step Word Problems to 1,000: Difference-Unknown
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1, 3.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-7
- **Description:** Use bar models to visualize and compare numbers. Given 2 numbers, find their difference. How much greater or how many more is the larger number? How much less or how many fewer is the smaller number? Start with learning problems and progress to story problems that provide context.

### 17. 1-Step Word Problems to 1,000: Larger-Unknown
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1, 3.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-8
- **Description:** Use bar models to find the larger number when the smaller number and difference are known. Describe the difference in an intuitive way using the word "more." Describe it in a less intuitive way using the word "less" or "fewer." Interpret and understand context to know which operation to use – addition or subtraction.

### 18. 1-Step Word Problems to 1,000: Smaller-Unknown
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1, 3.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-9
- **Description:** Use bar models to find the smaller number when the larger number and difference are known. Describe the difference in an intuitive way using the word "fewer." Describe the difference in a less intuitive way using the word "more." Interpret and understand context to know which operation to use – addition or subtraction.

### 19. 2-Step Word Problems to 1,000: Join & Separate
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1, 3.OA.8
- **Prerequisites:** 2-67 ○ 2-66, 2-65
- **Description:** Model 2-step word problems that require part-whole thinking in both steps. In the first step, join 2 parts and find their total. In the second step, add a third part and find the total of all 3 parts, or subtract a third part and find how many are left. For clarity, draw a separate bar model for each step.

### 20. 2-Step Word Problems to 1,000: Difference-Unknown
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1, 3.OA.8
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-16
- **Description:** Model 2-step, part-total and comparison problems. Draw stacked bars to visualize both steps on the same model. In the first step, use the total and one part to find the other part. In the second step, compare the parts and find their difference – how many more or fewer in one part than in the other.

### 21. 2-Step Word Problems to 1,000: Total-Unknown
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1, 3.OA.8
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-20
- **Description:** Model 2-step word problems that use the comparison words "more" and "fewer" in both intuitive and non-intuitive ways. In the first step, given one number and how many more or fewer it is than another number, find the other number. In the second step, add both numbers to find their sum, or total.

### 22. Place Value to 1,000: Rename Hundreds as Tens
- **Standards:** 3.NBT.2, 3.NBT.3
- **Prerequisites:** 3-10 ○ 3-1
- **Description:** Think flexibly about hundreds. How many tens in 123? Since 1 hundred = 10 tens, there are (10+2) tens = 12 tens in 123. How many tens in 267? Since 2 hundred is (2 groups of 10) tens = 20 tens, there are (20+6) tens = 26 tens in 267. How many tens in 389? Using mental math, 3 hundreds in 30 tens, so there are 38 tens.

### 23. Rounding 2-Digit Numbers to the Nearest Ten
- **Standards:** 3.NBT.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-22
- **Description:** Use an open number line and bar model to determine if a number is closer to the number of tens it has, or 1 more ten. What's 23 rounded to the nearest ten? The nearest multiples of ten are 20 and 30 – 2 tens and 3 tens. Halfway between 20 and 30 is 25. Since 23 is less than 25, it's closer to 2 tens. Round down to 20.

### 24. Rounding 3-Digit Numbers to the Nearest Ten
- **Standards:** 3.NBT.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-23
- **Description:** Generalize by determining if a 3-digit number is closer to the number of tens it has, or 1 more ten. What's 286 rounded to the nearest ten? The nearest multiples of ten are 280 and 290 – 28 tens and 29 tens. Halfway between 280 and 290 is 285. 286 is greater than 285, so it's closer to 29 tens. Round up to 290.

### 25. Rounding 3-Digit Numbers to the Nearest Hundred
- **Standards:** 3.NBT.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-24
- **Description:** Generalize by determining if a 3-digit number is closer to the number of hundreds it has, or to 1 more hundred. What's 273 rounded to the nearest hundred? The nearest multiples of a hundred are 2 hundreds and 3 hundreds – 200 and 300. Halfway between is 250. Since 273 is greater than 250, round up to 300.

### 26. Place Value to 10,000: Expanded Form*
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-22 ○ 2-76
- **Description:** Enrich by modeling 4-digit and 5-digit numbers. The pattern for ones – 1, 10, 100 – continues with thousands. The place values from right-to-left are: 1 one, 10 ones, 100 ones, 1 thousand, 10 thousands, 100 thousands. A comma separates the ones and thousands. What is 23,456? It's (2 x 10,000) + (3 x 1,000) + (4 x 100) + (5 x 10) + (6 x 1) = 20,000 + 3,000 + 400 + 50 + 6. *Worksheets Updated 9/16/24

## 3. Multiply 0-5 Groups

### 27. Multiply Equal Groups using Arrays
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-135
- **Description:** Use arrays to model and find the total of 2, 3, 4, and 5 equal groups. 2 groups of 6 is how many in all? Since 6+6 = 12, 2 groups of 6 = 12. How many groups? 2. How many in each group? 6. Focus on the important difference between the number of equal groups and the size of each group.

### 28. Multiply 0-1 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-27
- **Description:** Use the multiplication symbol "x" to mean "groups of." What's 1x2? Since 1 group of 2 is 2, 1x2 = 2. 1 group of any number is the number itself - the identity property of multiplication. What's 0x2? Since 0 groups of 2 is 0, 0x2 = 0. To visualize 0 groups, start with the 1 group and take it away. What's left? 0 groups, which is always zero.

### 29. Multiply 2-3 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-28
- **Description:** Use a bar model to represent the total of 2 or 3 equal groups. What's 2x2, or 2 groups of 2? "A group of 2? It's no trouble. Just make sure you always double." 2 doubled is 4 so 2x2 = 2+2 = 4. What's 3x5, or 3 groups of 5? "A group of 3 is quickly done. Start with 2 and then add 1." Since 2x5 = 10 and 10+5 = 15, 3x5 = 15.

### 30. Multiply 4 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-29
- **Description:** Use a bar model to figure out how many in all with 4 equal groups. What's 4x2, or 4 groups of 2? Think of 4 as 2 and 2. Since 4 groups of 2 is 2 groups of 2 and 2 groups of 2, 4x2 = 2x2 + 2x2 = 4+4 = 8. How many equal groups? 4. How many in each group? 2. "A group of 4 is fast to do, if you think in groups of 2."

### 31. Multiply 5 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-30
- **Description:** Use a bar model to figure out how many in all with 5 equal groups. What's 5x4, or 5 groups of 4? Think of 5 as 3 and 2. Since 5 groups of 4 is 3 groups of 4 and 2 groups of 4, 5x4 = 3x4 + 2x4 = 12+8 = 20. How many equal groups? 5. How many in each group? 4. "A group of 5 is fast to do. Start with 3. Then add 2."

### 32. Multiply 2-5 Groups Fluently with Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4, 3.OA.7
- **Prerequisites:** 3-31
- **Description:** Work toward fluency with multiplication facts by moving from bar models to number bonds. What's 4x7? Use a number bond to break 4 into 2 and 2. Apply the distributive property to find and add partial products to get the total product. 2x7 + 2x7 = 14+14 = 28, so 4x7 = 28. Multiply by breaking numbers apart in different ways.

### 33. Multiply 2-5 Groups Fluently with Equations
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4, 3.OA.7
- **Prerequisites:** 3-32
- **Description:** Transition from using number bonds to writing equations and using mental math. What's 3x5? Visualize 3 groups of 5 as 2 groups of 5 and 1 group of 5. Since 2x5 = 10 and 1x5 = 5, 3x5 = 10+5 = 15. Find and add partial products to get the total product – the distributive property. The goal is fluency first, then automaticity.

### 34. Multiply 2-5 Groups: Equal Groups Story Problems
- **Standards:** 3.OA.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-33
- **Description:** Use and interpret story problems to provide context for multiplication. Given the number of equal groups and the size of each group, find the product, or total. Use bar models to visualize the relationship between the parts and total. Apply the distributive property to find and add partial products to get the total product.

### 35. Multiply 2-5 Groups: Geometric Measurement
- **Standards:** 3.MD.8
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-90
- **Description:** Provide context for multiplication by finding the perimeter of equilateral triangles, squares, and regular pentagons. If the sides of a triangle all measure 2 inches, what's the perimeter? Since the total length of all 3 sides is 3x2 = 2x2 + 1x2 = 6, the perimeter is 6 inches. Find and add partial lengths to get the total length.

### 36. Multiply 2-5 Groups: Perimeter Problems
- **Standards:** 3.OA.3, 3.MD.8
- **Prerequisites:** 3-35, 3-34
- **Description:** Use bar models to find the perimeter of equilateral triangles, squares, and regular pentagons. Given the shape and side lengths measured in inches, feet, centimeters, or meters, find the perimeter. Use a bar model to show the relationship between the number of sides, the length of each side, and the perimeter.

## 4. Multiply 6-10 Groups

### 37. Explore Patterns by Skip Counting - 2 to 5
- **Standards:** 3.OA.9
- **Prerequisites:** 2-22
- **Description:** Use patterns to build familiarity with the basic times tables. Given a rule, write the pattern. Given a pattern, write the rule. To extend a pattern, skip count by using repeated addition. To build number sense and develop algebraic thinking skills, reason about the sum of even numbers and the sum of even and odd numbers.

### 38. Explore Patterns by Skip Counting - 6 to 9
- **Standards:** 3.OA.9
- **Prerequisites:** 3-37
- **Description:** Continue to use patterns to reinforce multiplication facts and develop algebraic thinking. If the rule is "Add 6" and the 6th number in the pattern is 36, what's the 8th number? Start by skip counting. Add 6, twice. 36+6 = 42, and 42+6 = 48. Progress to using the distributive property. Since 6x6 = 36 and 2x6 = 12, 8x6 = 48.

### 39. Introduce the Ellipsis Symbol ( ••• ) in Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-34
- **Description:** Use an ellipsis as a shortcut when drawing 5 or more groups. What's 5x4? Instead of drawing all 5 groups, draw groups 1 and 2 with 4 in each group. Draw a bar with "•••" to show the pattern of 4s continues, then draw the last 4 – group 5. Which groups does the "•••" represent? The ellipsis represents groups 3 and 4.

### 40. Multiply 6 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-39
- **Description:** Use a bar model to visualize 6 equal groups. Start by explicitly drawing all 6 groups. What's 6x4? Find and add partial products to get the total product. "A group of 6 is quick to see, if you think in groups of 3." 6x4 = 3x4 + 3x4 = 12+12 = 24. Transition to modeling 6 groups with an ellipsis. Use "•••" to represent groups 3 to 5.

### 41. Multiply 7 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-40
- **Description:** Use a bar model to visualize 7 equal groups. Start by explicitly drawing all 7 groups. What's 7x5? Find and add partial products to get the total product. "A group of 7 can be quick. 5 and 2 will do the trick." 7x5 = 5x5 + 2x5 = 25+10 = 35. Transition to modeling 7 groups with an ellipsis. Use "•••" to represent groups 3 to 6.

### 42. Multiply 8 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-41
- **Description:** Use a bar model to visualize 8 equal groups. Start by drawing all 8 groups. What's 8x3? Find and add partial products to get the total product. "A group of 8 is nothing more, than equal groups of 4 and 4." 8x3 = 4x3 + 4x3 = 12+12 = 24. Transition to showing 8 groups with an ellipsis. Use "•••" to represent groups 3 to 7.

### 43. Multiply 9 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-42
- **Description:** Use a bar model to visualize 9 equal groups. Start by drawing all 9 groups. What's 9x4? Find and add partial products to get the total product. "A group of 9 is nothing more, than smaller groups of 5 and 4." 9x4 = 5x4 + 4x4 = 20+16 = 36. Transition to modeling 9 groups with an ellipsis. Use "•••" to represent groups 3 to 8.

### 44. Multiply 10 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-43
- **Description:** Use a bar model to visualize 10 equal groups. Start by drawing all 10 groups. What's 10x3? Find and add partial products to get the total product. "A group of 10? It's no trouble. Start with 5 and then just double." 10x3 = 5x3 + 5x3 = 15+15 = 30. Transition to modeling 10 groups by using "•••" to represent groups 3 to 9.

### 45. Multiply 6-10 Groups using Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4, 3.OA.7
- **Prerequisites:** 3-44 ○ 3-32
- **Description:** Work toward fluency by using number bonds instead of bar models. What's 6x6? 6 groups are 3 groups and 3 groups. Since 3x6 = 18, 6x6 = 18+18 = 36. 6 groups are also 5 groups plus 1 group. Since 5x6 = 30 and 1x6 = 6, 6x6 = 30+6 = 36. Explore smart ways to break numbers apart and build computational fluency.

### 46. Multiply 6-10 Groups using Mental Math
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4, 3.OA.7
- **Prerequisites:** 3-45
- **Description:** Transition from number bonds to using equations and mental math. What's 9x7? 9 groups are 5 groups and 4 groups. Since 5x7 = 35 and 4x7 = 28, 9x7 = 35+28 = 63. 9 groups are also 10 groups minus 1 group. Since 10x7 = 70, 9x7 = 70-7 = 63. Build computational fluency by breaking numbers apart in different ways.

### 47. Multiply 6-10 Groups: Equal-Groups Story Problems
- **Standards:** 3.OA.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-39
- **Description:** Use and interpret story problems to provide context for multiplication. Given the number of equal groups and the size of each group, find the product, or total. Use bar models to visualize the relationship between the parts and total. Apply the distributive property to find and add partial products to get the total product.

### 48. Multiply 6-10 Groups with Picture Graphs
- **Standards:** 3.MD.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-130
- **Description:** Use scaled picture graphs to master multiplication facts, visualize data with larger values, and provide context for operations. Use the graph's key to know the value of each picture in the graph. If a pumpkin has a value of 2, what is the value of 10 pumpkins? Since 10x2 = 20, 10 pumpkins have a value of 20.

### 49. Multiply 6-10 Groups with Bar Graphs
- **Standards:** 3.MD.3
- **Prerequisites:** 2-131
- **Description:** Generalize from picture graphs to scaled bar graphs. Represent equal groups with unit bars. Calculate the total number of objects in each category by multiplying the number of unit bars by the value of each unit – given in the graph's key. Visually, the number of objects in a category is represented by the length of its bar.

### 50. Multiply 6-10 Groups in Perimeter Problems Part I
- **Standards:** 3.MD.8
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-35
- **Description:** Reinforce multiplication facts by finding the perimeter of regular hexagons, heptagons, octagons, and nonagons. If a hexagon's sides all measure 9 inches, what's its perimeter? Since the total length of 6 sides is 6x9 = 3x9 + 3x9 = 27+27 = 54, the perimeter is 54 inches. Find and add partial lengths to get the total length.

### 51. Multiply 6-10 Groups in Perimeter Problems Part II
- **Standards:** 3.OA.3, 3.MD.8
- **Prerequisites:** 3-36
- **Description:** Use bar models to find the perimeter of regular hexagons, heptagons, octagons, and nonagons. Given the shape and side lengths measured in inches, feet, centimeters, or meters, find the perimeter. Use a bar model to show the relationship between the number of sides, the length of each side, and the perimeter.

## 5. Divide by Sharing

### 52. Divide-by-Sharing with Arrays
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-27
- **Description:** Introduce division by sharing. Divide a total - shown as an array - into equal groups. Find the size of each group. If 12 is divided into 2 equal groups, how many are in each group? Think multiplication or repeated addition. 2 groups of what number is 12? Since 2x6 = 6+6 = 12, there are 6 in each group. 12÷2 = 6.

### 53. Divide-by-Sharing 0-1 Groups
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-52
- **Description:** Introduce the division symbol and divide by sharing. Interpret the divisor as the number of equal groups, and the quotient as the number in each group. What's 5÷1? Divide 5 into 1 group. Since all 5 are in that group, 5÷1 = 5. What's 5÷0? Since we can't divide 5 into 0 groups, the answer is "we can't divide by 0" or "undefined."

### 54. Divide-by-Sharing 2-3 Groups with Arrays & Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2, 3.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 3-53
- **Description:** Use the sharing model to divide a total into 2 or 3 equal groups. Use a bar model to visualize the number of groups, size of each group, and total. What's 6÷3? What's 6 divided into 3 equal groups? Think multiplication. 3 groups of how many is 6? Since 3x2 = 2+2+2 = 6, 6÷3 = 2. There are 3 equal groups, with 2 in each group.

### 55. Divide-by-Sharing 4-5 Groups with Arrays & Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2, 3.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 3-54
- **Description:** Generalize from 2 or 3 equal groups to dividing into 4 or 5 equal groups. Use a bar model to visualize the number of groups, size of each group, and total. What's 8÷4 = 2. What's 8 divided into 4 equal groups? 4 groups of how many is 8? Since 4x2 = 8, 8÷4 = 2. There are 4 equal groups, with 2 in each group.

### 56. Divide-by-Sharing 6-9 Groups with Arrays & Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2, 3.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 3-55
- **Description:** Generalize from 4 or 5 equal groups to dividing into 6, 7, 8, or 9 equal groups. Use a bar model to visualize the number of groups, size of each group, and total. What's 21÷7? What's 21 divided into 7 equal groups? 7 groups of how many is 21? Since 7x3 = 21, 21÷7 = 3. There are 7 equal groups, with 3 in each group.

### 57. Divide-by-Sharing 2-5 Groups with Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4, 3.OA.7
- **Prerequisites:** 3-56

## 6. Divide by Grouping

### 65. Divide-by-Grouping using Arrays
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-52
- **Description:** Introduce division by grouping. Divide a total - modeled by an array - into equal groups. Find the number of groups. If 12 is divided into groups of 6, how many groups of 6 are there? Think multiplication or repeated addition. How many groups of 6 make 12? Since 6x2 = 6+6 = 12, there are 2 groups of 6 and 12÷6 = 2.

### 66. Divide-by-Grouping: Groups of 0-1
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-65
- **Description:** Model division by grouping instead of by sharing. Interpret the divisor as the size of each group, and the quotient as the number of equal groups. What's 5÷1? What's 5 divided into groups of 1? Since 5x1 = 5, 5÷1 = 5. What's 5÷0? Since no amount of 0s can make 5, the answer is "we can't divide by 0" or "undefined."

### 67. Divide by Grouping: Groups of 2-5 with Arrays & Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2, 3.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 3-66
- **Description:** Use the grouping model to divide. Interpret the divisor as the size of each group, not the number of groups. What's 8÷4? What's 8 divided into equal groups of 4? Multiply or add to find how many groups of 4 are in 8. Since 2x4 = 4+4 = 8, 8÷4 = 2. Use an ellipsis to model an unknown number of groups with a bar model.

### 68. Divide-by-Grouping: Groups of 6-9 with Arrays & Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2, 3.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 3-67
- **Description:** Use the grouping model to divide with more equal groups. What's 24÷4, 24 divided into equal groups of 4? Multiply to find how many groups of 4 are in 24. Since 6x4 = 24, 24÷4 = 6. When the dividend gets large, another strategy is to break it into smaller parts. What's 35÷5? Since 25÷5 = 5 and 10÷5 = 2, 35÷5 = 5+2 = 7.

### 69. Divide-by-Grouping: Groups of 2-5 with Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2, 3.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 3-68
- **Description:** Work toward fluency with division facts. Use the grouping model of division with up to 5 equal groups. What's 14÷7? What's 14 divided into groups of 7? Scaffold from known multiplication facts to learn and master unknown division facts. Since 2x7 = 14, 14÷7 = 2. Division is multiplication with an unknown factor.

### 70. Divide-by-Grouping: Groups of 6-9 with Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2, 3.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 3-69
- **Description:** Continue working toward fluency with division facts. Use the grouping model of division with up to 9 equal groups. What's 56÷8, 56 divided into equal groups of 8? How many groups of 8 = 56? Scaffold from known multiplication facts to learn and master unknown division facts. Since 5x8 = 40, 2x8 = 16, 7x8 = 56, and 56÷8 = 7.

### 71. Divide-by-Grouping Fluently
- **Standards:** 3.OA.7
- **Prerequisites:** 3-70
- **Description:** Continue to work toward fluency within 100 while laying the foundation for dividing larger totals (dividends). What's 21÷3? Apply the distributive property to find and add partial quotients to get the total quotient. Since 21 = 15+6, 21÷3 = (15+6)÷3 = (15÷3)+(6÷3) = 5+2 = 7. Break the dividend into a multiple of 5 and what's left.

### 72. Divide-by-Grouping in Number Patterns
- **Standards:** 3.OA.9
- **Prerequisites:** 3-71
- **Description:** Use patterns to reinforce division by grouping. If a pattern of 4 shapes keeps repeating, how many times will it repeat if there are 12 shapes in all? What's 12÷4? How many 4s are in 12? Since 3x4 = 12, 12÷4 = 3 and the 4-shape pattern repeats 3 times. What's the 12th shape in the pattern? The 4th shape in the repeating pattern.

### 73. Divide-by-Grouping Story Problems
- **Standards:** 3.OA.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-70 ○ 3-61
- **Description:** Use story problems to provide context for the grouping model of division. Given the total and size of each group, find the number of equal groups. Use bar models to visualize the relationship between the parts and total. Use the relationship between division and multiplication to figure out how many equal groups.

### 74. Divide-by-Grouping Perimeter Problems
- **Standards:** 3.OA.3, 3.MD.8
- **Prerequisites:** 3-64 ○ 3-73
- **Description:** Divide by grouping to find how many sides and the name of a regular polygon. If a shape's perimeter is 30 and each side is 6, what's the name of the shape? Draw a bar model to see 30 is the total and 6 is the size of each group. How many groups? Since 5x6 = 30 and 30÷6 = 5, there are 5 sides. The shape is a pentagon.

## 7. Fraction Foundations

### 75. Model Fractions using Linear Models
- **Standards:** 3.NF.1, 3.G.2
- **Prerequisites:** 2-56 ○ 2-53
- **Description:** Divide bar models into halves, thirds, fourths, sixths, and eighths. Draw equal parts in smart ways. Divide halves into halves to make fourths, halves into thirds and thirds into halves to make sixths, and fourths into halves and halves into fourths to make eighths. Shade both contiguous and non-contiguous parts (gaps).

### 76. Model Fractions using Area Models
- **Standards:** 3.NF.1, 3.G.2
- **Prerequisites:** 2-57
- **Description:** Transition from dividing the length of a bar into equal parts, to dividing the area of a square or circle into equal parts. Divide halves into fourths, sixths, and eighths, thirds into sixths, and fourths into eighths. Shade adjacent parts, or shade non-adjacent parts with gaps or spaces between them.

### 77. Fractions of the Whole
- **Standards:** 3.NF.1, 3.G.2
- **Prerequisites:** 2-59
- **Description:** Divide equilateral triangles, squares, regular hexagons, and regular octagons into equal parts. Draw halves, thirds, fourths, sixths, and eighths by drawing connecting lines from a dot in the center to 2 or more of the vertices.

### 78. Fractions of Fractions
- **Standards:** 3.NF.1, 3.G.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-77
- **Description:** Generalize from dividing the whole into equal parts, to dividing fractions of the whole into equal parts. Draw fourths? Divide halves in half. Sixths? Divide thirds in half, or halves in thirds. Eighths? Divide fourths in half or halves in fourths. Partition triangles, trapezoids, rhombuses, hexagons, and octagons into fractional parts.

### 79. Fraction Notation
- **Standards:** 3.NF.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-77
- **Description:** If a whole is divided into 3 equal parts, 1 part is "1 third" and written "1/3". 2 parts are "2 thirds" or "2/3". The NUMerator gives the number of parts. The deNOMinator gives the name of the parts and number of equal parts in the whole. Match fractions of circles and regular polygons to written fraction notation.

### 80. Unit Fractions (1/b)
- **Standards:** 3.NF.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-75 ○ 2-53
- **Description:** Show unit fractions on a number line. What's 1/8? It's the length of 1 part – measured as the distance from 0 – when the length 1 is divided into 8 equal parts. Draw a bar to represent the length 1 as the distance between 0 and 1. Divide the bar into 8 equal parts. Shade the first one and write 1/8 on the number line.

### 81. Proper Fractions (a/b < 1)
- **Standards:** 3.NF.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-80
- **Description:** Show proper fractions on a number line. What's 7/8? It's the length of 7 parts – measured as the distance from 0 – when the length 1 is divided into 8 equal parts. Draw a bar to represent 1 as the distance between 0 and 1. Divide the bar into 8 equal parts. Shade the first seven and write 7/8 on the number line.

### 82. Improper Fractions (a/b ≥ 1)
- **Standards:** 3.NF.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-81
- **Description:** Show "fractions greater than 1" on a number line. What's 7/6? It's 6 sixths plus 1 sixth. Draw and divide a bar between 0 and 1 into sixths. Shade all 6, or 6/6. Draw and divide a bar between 1 and 2 into sixths. Shade the first part, or 1/6. How much is shaded in all? 7 sixths. Write 7/6 to show the total distance from 0.

### 83. Inverse Relationship between the Number & Size of Equal Parts
- **Standards:** 3.NF.3
- **Prerequisites:** 2-54
- **Description:** Use bar models to compare the size of unit fractions. On a number line, show larger denominators mean more equal parts so each part is smaller. Smaller denominators mean fewer equal parts so each part is larger. Large denominators have smaller parts. Small denominators have larger parts. It is called an "inverse relationship."

### 84. Equivalent Fractions converting to More, Smaller Parts
- **Standards:** 3.NF.3
- **Prerequisites:** 3-83
- **Description:** Draw bar models to find equivalent fractions on a number line. 1 half is how many sixths? Divide the distance between 0 and 1 into 2 equal parts. Shade 1 part, or 1/2. To make sixths, partition each half into 3 equal parts. Since there are now 6 equal parts in 1, each part is 1 sixth, or 1/6. 3 sixths are shaded, so 1/2 = 3/6.

### 85. Equivalent Fractions converting to Fewer, Larger Parts
- **Standards:** 3.NF.3
- **Prerequisites:** 3-84
- **Description:** Generalize from finding more parts, to finding more challenging, equivalent fractions with fewer parts. 4 eighths is how many halves? Divide 1 – the distance between 0 and 1 – into 8 equal parts. Shade 4 parts, or 4/8. Think of the shaded 4/8 as 1 part. How many are in 1? Since there are 2, each is 1 half, and 4/8 = 1/2.

### 86. Compare Fractions with Equal Numerators
- **Standards:** 3.NF.3
- **Prerequisites:** 3-83
- **Description:** Draw and compare fractions. When fractions have the same number of parts – common, like, equal, or same numerators – compare the size of parts – denominators. Which is greater, 3/4 or 3/6? Both have 3 parts, but fourths are larger, so 3/4 > 3/6. Which is less, 2/4 or 2/3? Both have 2 parts. Fourths are smaller, so 2/4 < 2/3.

### 87. Compare Fractions with Equal Denominators
- **Standards:** 3.NF.3
- **Prerequisites:** 3-86
- **Description:** Compare fractions on a number line. When parts are the same size – common, like, or equal denominators – compare the number of parts – numerators. Which is less, 4/6 or 5/6? Both have sixths, but four is less than 5, so 4/6 < 5/6. Which is greater, 7/8 or 6/8? Both have eighths, but seven is greater than 6, so 7/8 > 6/8.

### 88. Order Fractions on a Number Line*
- **Standards:** 3.NF.2, 3.NF.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-87 ○ 3-86, 3-83
- **Description:** Use common numerator and denominator strategies to order fractions on a number line. Same numerators? Compare denominators. Same denominators? Compare numerators. For enrichment, compare fractions to the benchmark fraction 1/2. Use reasoning to know if a fraction is greater or less than 1/2.

## 10. Length, Graphing, Line Plots

### 118. Measure & Compare Length to the nearest Whole Unit
- **Standards:** 3.MD.4
- **Prerequisites:** 2-114
- **Description:** Use rulers to measure and compare colored bars. Find the difference between 2 bars by adding and subtracting in inches or centimeters. Align bars with zero, then transition to calculating length as the distance between any 2 points. Use rulers like a number line.

### 119. Measure & Compare Length to the nearest Half Unit
- **Standards:** 3.MD.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-118
- **Description:** Transition from whole units to half-unit lengths. Measure a bar's length to the nearest half paper clip or half inch. Start by measuring from the first paper clip or from zero on the ruler. Progress to starting from any point. Reinforce length as the distance between 2 points.

### 120. Measure & Compare Length to the nearest Quarter Unit
- **Standards:** 3.MD.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-119
- **Description:** Measure a bar's length to the nearest quarter paper clip or quarter inch. Start by measuring from the first paper clip or from zero on the ruler. Progress to starting from any point. Encourage students to see and understand length as the distance between 2 points.

### 121. Scaled Picture Graphs
- **Standards:** 3.MD.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-48 ○ 2-130
- **Description:** Introduce scaled picture graphs to visualize data with larger values. Scale the value of each picture by the factor given in the graph's key. Quantify and compare categories, both in units and in values. Use scaled graphs to develop fluency with multiplication facts and encourage proportional reasoning.

### 122. Scaled Bar Graphs
- **Standards:** 3.MD.3
- **Prerequisites:** 3-49 ○ 2-131
- **Description:** Transition to more flexible, bar graphs. Draw unit bars to represent scaled values. The number of objects in each category is represented by the length of each bar, and calculated by scaling the number of units by the factor in the key. Compare and combine categories to find totals, sums, and differences.

### 123. Line Plots to the nearest Whole Number
- **Standards:** 3.MD.4
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-133
- **Description:** Plot the frequency of length data measured to the nearest whole unit. What's the smallest value – the minimum? What's the largest value – the maximum? Which value is observed the most – the mode? Compare the frequency of specific values to gain a better understanding of a sample's data and how it is distributed.

### 124. Line Plots to the nearest Halves & Fourths
- **Standards:** 3.MD.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-123
- **Description:** Plot the frequency of length data measured to the nearest half and quarter inch. What's the smallest value (minimum)? Largest value (maximum)? Which value is observed the most (mode)? Compare the frequency of specific values to gain a better understanding of how the sample's data is distributed.

## 11. Time, Weight, Liquid Volume

### 125. Read & Write Time to the Nearest Minute
- **Standards:** 3.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-126
- **Description:** Start by reading time on an analog clock to the nearest 1 minute. What time is it when the hour hand is between 9 and 10 and the minute hand shows 12 minutes after the hour? 9 hours and 12 minutes have elapsed since 12:00, so it's "nine twelve" or "12 minutes after 9." Progress to drawing hour and minute hands.

### 126. Elapsed Time Within the Hour: End-Time-Unknown
- **Standards:** 3.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-125 ○ 2-40, 2-36
- **Description:** Use a timeline to solve elapsed time problems by visualizing the start time, elapsed time, and end time. Start by finding the end time – result unknown problems – when the end time and start time are within the same hour. If the start time is 1:08 and elapsed time is :17, what is the end time? It's 1:08 + :17 = 1:25.

### 127. Elapsed Time Within the Hour: Elapsed-Time-Unknown
- **Standards:** 3.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-126 ○ 2-41, 2-37
- **Description:** Find the elapsed time – change unknown problems – when the end time and start time are within the same hour. If the start time is 2:05 and the end time is 2:26, what is the elapsed time? Solve 2:05 + ? = 2:26, or 2:26 - ? = 2:05. Add or subtract in 1 or more steps to find the difference. The elapsed time is :21.

### 128. Elapsed Time Within the Hour: Start-Time-Unknown
- **Standards:** 3.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-127 ○ 2-42, 2-38
- **Description:** Find the start time – start unknown problems – when the end time and start time are within the same hour. If the elapsed time is :14 and the end time is 3:35, what is the start time? Work backward. The start time is 3:35 - :14 = 3:35 - :10 - :04 = 3:25 - :04 = 3:21. Check for accuracy by adding. 3:21 + :14 = 3:35.

### 129. Elapsed Time Bridge the Hour: End-Time-Unknown
- **Standards:** 3.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-126
- **Description:** Find the end time – result unknown problems – when the end and start times are in different hours. If the start time is 1:56 and the elapsed time is :38, what is the end time? Think of :38 as :04 and :34. First add :04 to get to 2:00, then add :34 more to get to 2:34. First make 60 – the next hour – then add the rest.

### 130. Elapsed Time Bridge the Hour: Elapsed-Time-Unknown
- **Standards:** 3.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-127
- **Description:** Find the elapsed time – change unknown problems – when the end and start times are in different hours. If the start time is 2:48 and the end time is 3:14, what is the elapsed time? First add :12 to get to 3:00. Need to add :14 more to get to 3:14, so the total elapsed time is :12 + :14 = :26. 2:48 + :26 = 3:14.

### 131. Elapsed Time Bridge the Hour: Start-Time-Unknown
- **Standards:** 3.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-128
- **Description:** Find the start time – start unknown problems – when the start and end times are in different hours. What's the start time if the elapsed time is :15 and the end time is 2:02? Work backward. First take away :02 to get to 2:00. Since :02 + :13 = :15, take away :13 more. The start time is 2:00 - :10 - :03 = 1:50 - :03 = 1:47.

### 132. Weight & Mass
- **Standards:** 3.MD.2
- **Description:** Read a scale to determine an object's weight in ounces and pounds, or its mass in grams and kilograms. Estimate the weight or mass of a second object by comparing it to the first object. Does a larger object always have more weight and mass? Why or why not? Can a larger object have less weight and mass?

### 133. Liquid Volume
- **Standards:** 3.MD.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-112, 3-108
- **Description:** Use liquid volume to provide context for operations. Multiply to find the total volume of liquid in up to 9 containers. Divide by sharing to find the volume of liquid in 1 container. Divide by grouping to find the number of containers with an equal volume of liquid. Measure in metric liters.

## 12. Geometry

### 134. Classify & Compare Shapes by Defining Attributes
- **Standards:** 3.G.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-134
- **Description:** Categorize shapes by identifying their defining attributes. If a shape has 4 equal sides, 2 pairs of parallel sides, and 4 square corners, what kind of shape is it? Can a shape have more than one name or category? It's a quadrilateral, rhombus, rectangle, and square. Goal is to commit names and attributes to memory.

### 135. Classify & Compare Shapes by Category
- **Standards:** 3.G.1
- **Prerequisites:** 3-134
- **Description:** Explore how categories are related by identifying shared attributes. Which defining attributes do a square and rhombus have in common? They both have 4 equal sides, so both are quadrilaterals and rhombuses. Since squares also have 4 square corners, they're a special kind of rhombus and called a subcategory.

## Overview

Third grade marks a significant shift, with four critical areas: developing understanding of multiplication and division (and strategies within 100); understanding fractions (especially unit fractions); understanding rectangular arrays and area; and describing/analyzing two-dimensional shapes. Students represent and solve multiplication and division word problems, understand properties (commutative, associative, distributive), become fluent with facts within 100, and solve multistep problems involving all four operations while identifying arithmetic patterns. Place-value work continues as they round whole numbers and use strategies for multidigit addition and subtraction. Fractions are introduced as numbers: students understand a fraction 1/b as the quantity formed by one part when a whole is partitioned into b equal parts, recognize and generate equivalent fractions, and compare fractions with the same numerator or denominator. They measure area by counting unit squares, relate area to multiplication and addition (including decomposing rectilinear figures), and solve problems involving perimeter. Geometry involves categorizing shapes by attributes (e.g., quadrilaterals, polygons) and partitioning shapes into equal areas. Data work includes scaled picture and bar graphs and measuring liquid volumes and masses. These concepts help students see mathematics as interconnected and applicable to everyday problems.
