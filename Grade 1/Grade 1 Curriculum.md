## 1. Tune Up

### 1. Quantify Numbers to 5
- **Standards:** K.CC.5
- **Prerequisites:** K-12
- **Description:** Model numbers on a five frame. Count, add, or subitize – figure out quickly without counting – how many and how many are missing and needed to make 5 in all. Start with static images. Progress to quick images that limit processing time and build number sense. Encourage grouping instead of counting.

### 2. Quantify Numbers to 10
- **Standards:** K.CC.5, K.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** K-59 ○ K-26, K-25
- **Description:** Model numbers on a ten frame. How many counters? Use multiple strategies including counting all, counting on, adding the rows, and adding groups within a group. How many frames are empty? Knowing how many are missing makes figuring out how many are there, easier. Review and master all the ways to make 10.

### 3. Subitize Numbers to 10
- **Standards:** K.CC.5, K.OA.4
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** K-68, K-67
- **Description:** Progress from static images, to finding combinations that make 10 on quick images. Show ten frames for 3 seconds or less to limit processing time, encourage grouping instead of counting, and build number sense. Use non-counting strategies to quickly figure out how many and how many more makes 10.

### 4. Horizontal Picture Graphs to 10
- **Standards:** K.CC.5, K.CC.6
- **Prerequisites:** K-20
- **Description:** Use picture graphs to model and compare numbers. Add or count on from 5 to figure out how many. Read the graph's axis to check for accuracy. Use keywords to compare categories, including "more" and "fewer" for countable objects, and "greater" and "less" for numbers. Use shapes, solids, and coins for counters.

### 5. Vertical Picture Graphs to 10
- **Standards:** K.CC.5, K.CC.6
- **Prerequisites:** 1-4 ○ K-21
- **Description:** Encourage flexibility by generalizing from horizontal to vertical picture graphs. Explore different ways to figure out how many. Emphasize grouping and adding strategies over counting strategies. Use shapes, solids, and U.S. coins for counters. Discuss their similarities, differences, and defining attributes.

### 6. Count Forward to 10
- **Standards:** K.CC.4
- **Prerequisites:** K-22
- **Description:** Put sets and digits in increasing order from 1 to 10. Use grouping strategies to figure out how many are in each set or group. When counting from 1 to 10, understand that each number represents a set with 1 more – not the last object counted. 8 is greater than 7. How much greater? 8 shapes are 1 more than 7 shapes.

### 7. Add Fluently within 5
- **Standards:** K.OA.5
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** K-32 ○ K-31, K-30
- **Description:** Use equations and five frames to add within 5. What's 4+1? Visualize 4 on a five frame. Add 1 more. How many in all? The five frame is full, so total is 5. Check for accuracy by using or drawing counters. When ready, add mentally without five frames. Goal is fluency – adding quickly, accurately, and flexibly, without counting.

### 8. Add within 10 using Number Bonds & Ten Frames
- **Standards:** K.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** K-78 ○ K-38
- **Description:** Use number bonds and ten frames to find the total. What's 5 and 3 more? Visualize 5 on a ten frame – the top row is full. Visualize 3 more below. How many in all? Since 2 are missing, the total is 8. Check for accuracy by drawing counters. Encourage students to check their thinking and make adjustments when needed.

### 9. Add-To Word Problems to 10
- **Standards:** K.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** K-49 ○ 1-8
- **Description:** Solve "result unknown" problems. Given a starting amount ("start") and amount that's added to it ("change"), find the total ("result"). Start with learning problems that teach explicitly and don't require interpretation. Use a diagram to visualize the relationship between the parts and total. Transition to contextual story problems.

### 10. Put-Together Word Problems to 10
- **Standards:** K.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** K-52, K-51 ○ 1-8
- **Description:** Given 2 parts, join them together to find the total. Start with explicit, learning problems that don't require interpretation. Use a visual model to show the relationship between the parts and total. If needed, use a ten frame to add the parts. When ready, transition to story problems that provide context.

### 11. Count Backward from 10
- **Standards:** K.CC.4
- **Prerequisites:** K-23 ○ K-65
- **Description:** Put sets and digits in decreasing order. Use grouping strategies to figure out how many are in each set. When counting down, understand that each number counted is 1 less than the previous number. 6 is 1 less than 7, but 6 shapes are 1 fewer than 7 shapes. Use "fewer" to compare countable objects.

### 12. Subtract Fluently within 5
- **Standards:** K.OA.5
- **Prerequisites:** K-47 ○ 1-7
- **Description:** Use equations and five frames to subtract within 5. What's 5-1? Visualize 5 – a full five frame. Take 1 away. How many are left? Since a five frame with 1 missing is 4, 5-1 = 4. Check for accuracy by using or drawing counters. When ready, subtract fluently without five frames – flexibly, accurately, and quickly.

### 13. Subtract within 10
- **Standards:** K.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** K-91 ○ K-95, K-48, K-43
- **Description:** Use number bonds and ten frames to subtract and find the unknown part. What's 9-3? Visualize 9 on a ten frame – a full top row and 4 more. Mentally cross off 3. There is 1 left. How many in all? 6, so 9-3 = 6. Check for accuracy by drawing counters. Encourage students to check their own thinking – metacognition.

### 14. Take-From Word Problems to 10
- **Standards:** K.OA.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** K-54 ○ 1-13, K-55
- **Description:** Use a visual model to solve a "take-from, result unknown" problem. Given the total (start) and how many are taken away (change), find how many are left (result). Use a visual model to show the relationship between the parts and total. Subtract or add to find the unknown part. Transition to story problems to provide context.

### 15. Take-Apart Word Problems to 10
- **Standards:** K.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** K-57 ○ 1-14, K-58
- **Description:** Solve "take-apart" problems with both parts unknown. Given the total, how many could be in one part? How many would be in the other part? Since there are many ways to break a number apart, there is more than one answer. Start with explicit learning problems. Transition to contextual, story problems that need interpretation.

## 2. Operations to 10

### 16. Add within 10 Total-Unknown
- **Standards:** 1.OA.8
- **Prerequisites:** K-79 ○ K-78, K-38
- **Description:** Use equations and ten frames to find the total. What's 6 and 3 more? Visualize 6 on a ten frame – the top row is full and 1 more. Visualize 3 more. How many in all? Since 1 is still missing, the total is 9. Check for accuracy by drawing counters. Encourage students to check their own thinking and make adjustments when needed.

### 17. Add within 10 Picture Graphs
- **Standards:** 1.MD.4
- **Prerequisites:** K-63 ○ K-64, K-21, K-20, 1-16
- **Description:** Use horizontal and vertical picture graphs to provide context for addition. Add, compare, subitize, and read the graph's axis to figure out how many in each category. How many in 2 categories? Write an equation for the sum and solve by grouping in smart ways.

### 18. Add within 10 Change-Unknown & Start-Unknown
- **Standards:** 1.OA.8
- **Prerequisites:** K-87, K-86 ○ K-80
- **Description:** Use ten frames to find an unknown part in an addition equation. 5 + __ = 7? Visualize 5 on a ten frame – a full top row. Now imagine 7 – a full top row and 2 more. What's the "change" – how many need to be added to 5 to make 7? Since 5+2 = 7, the part needing to be added is 2. Draw to check for accuracy.

### 19. Subtract within 10 Result-Unknown
- **Standards:** 1.OA.8
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-13 ○ K-96, K-95, K-94
- **Description:** Use equations and ten frames to subtract. What's 8 minus 3? Visualize a total of 8 on a ten frame – a full top row and 3 more. Imagine crossing off all 3. How many are left? There are still 5 left, so 8-3 = 5. Check for accuracy by drawing and crossing off counters. Make adjustments when needed.

### 20. Subtract within 10 Picture Graphs
- **Standards:** 1.MD.4
- **Prerequisites:** K-89 ○ 1-19, K-63
- **Description:** Use picture graphs to provide context for subtraction. Figure out how many in each category, then find the difference between any 2 categories. How many more or extra in the larger category? How many fewer or missing in the smaller category? Write an equation and find each difference.

### 21. Subtract within 10 Change-Unknown & Start-Unknown
- **Standards:** 1.OA.8
- **Prerequisites:** 1-18 ○ K-92, K-91
- **Description:** Use ten frames to find an unknown part in a subtraction equation. 9 - __ = 6? Visualize a total of 9 on a ten frame – a full top row and 4 more. Now imagine 6. What's the "change" – how many need to be taken away from 9 to make 6? since 9-3 = 6, the part taken away is 3. Check for accuracy by drawing.

### 22. Meaning of the Equal Sign
- **Standards:** 1.OA.7
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** K-45
- **Description:** Reinforce the meaning of "equal" by comparing the value of what is on the left side of the equal sign, to the value of what is on the right side. Is the equation "3 = 10-6" true or false? Since the value of 3 is 3, and the value of 10-6 is 4, the equation is false. The 2 sides of the equal sign do not have the same value.

### 23. Balance Equations - Add within 10
- **Standards:** 1.OA.7, 1.OA.8
- **Prerequisites:** 1-22 ○ 1-18, 1-16
- **Description:** Solve addition equations with expressions on both sides of the equal sign. Use ten frames to model both change unknown and start unknown problems. Read "9 = 2 + __" as "9 has the same value as 2 and how many more?" Read "8 = __ + 3" as "8 has the same value as how many and 3 more?"

### 24. Balance Equations - Subtract within 10
- **Standards:** 1.OA.7, 1.OA.8
- **Prerequisites:** K-93 ○ 1-21, 1-19
- **Description:** Solve equations that have an expression on the right side of the equal sign, or on both sides. Use ten frames to model change unknown problems with an unknown part, and start unknown problems with an unknown total. Encourage students to practice and see how addition can be used to subtract.

### 25. Greater & Less Than Symbols
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** K-102
- **Description:** Introduce the greater than (>) and less than (<) symbols. Learn which symbol to draw by using a connect-the-dots method that makes sense of both symbols and the equal sign. Start by drawing and comparing numbers to 5. Practice by comparing numbers to 10.

### 26. Compare Expressions
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.3, 1.OA.7
- **Prerequisites:** 1-25 ○ 1-21, 1-19, 1-18, 1-16
- **Description:** Compare addition and subtraction expressions. Use ten frames to model, simplify, and find the value of each expression. When the values are not the same, use a greater than (>) or less than (<) symbol to show their relationship. When the values are the same, use an equal sign (=).

### 27. Add-To Change-Unknown Word Problems to 10
- **Standards:** 1.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-9 ○ 1-18
- **Description:** Given the starting amount (start) and ending amount (result), find how many were added (change). Use a part-total model to visually show the relationship between the parts and total. Find the unknown part by adding or subtracting. Start with learning problems. Transition to contextual story problems.

### 28. Add-To Start-Unknown Word Problems to 10
- **Standards:** 1.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-27 ○ 1-18
- **Description:** Given how many were added (change) and the ending amount (result), find the starting amount (start). Use a part-total model to visually show the relationship between the parts and total. Find the unknown part by adding or subtracting. Start with learning problems. Transition to contextual story problems.

### 29. Take-From Change-Unknown Word Problems to 10
- **Standards:** 1.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-21 ○ 1-13
- **Description:** Given the starting amount (start) and ending amount (result), find how many were taken away (change). Use a part-total model to visually show the relationship between the parts and total. Find the unknown part by adding or subtracting. Start with learning problems. Transition to contextual story problems.

### 30. Take-From Start-Unknown Word Problems to 10
- **Standards:** 1.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-29 ○ 1-21
- **Description:** Given how many were taken away (change) and the ending amount (result), find the starting amount (start). Use a part-total model to visually show the relationship between the parts and total. Find the unknown total by adding. Start with learning problems. Transition to contextual story problems.

## 3. Teen Numbers

### 31. Subitize Teen Numbers using Ten Frames
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** K-109 ○ K-103, K-68
- **Description:** Start by modeling teen numbers visually on static ten frames. Progress to quick images that encourage grouping and subitizing – figuring out how many quickly without counting – by limiting processing time. Subitize daily to develop number sense and lay the groundwork for place value.

### 32. Compare Teen Numbers
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.3
- **Prerequisites:** K-108 ○ K-102
- **Description:** Use a "compare-the-parts" strategy to build number sense, encourage part-whole-thinking, and get ready to quantify the difference between 2 numbers. Is 13 greater than 11? Both have a full ten frame, so compare what's left. 3 is greater than 1, so 13 is greater than 11. Model numbers with ten frames, then number bonds.

### 33. Even vs Odd*
- **Standards:** 1.OA.5, 2.OA.3
- **Description:** Enrich by comparing a set of numbers and determine if the largest and smallest numbers are even or odd. To determine if a number is even or odd, model it with a row of evenly spaced dots. If every dot can be paired with a partner, the number is even. If every dot cannot be paired and there is 1 extra, the number is odd.

### 34. Quantify and Compare Teen Numbers using Linear Models
- **Standards:** 1.MD.4
- **Prerequisites:** K-100 ○ K-99
- **Description:** Use linear models to compare and quantify the difference between 2 groups. When comparing countable objects, use the comparison words "more" and "fewer." How many more in the larger group? Count the extra objects. How many fewer in the smaller group? Count the objects that are "missing."

### 35. Quantify and Compare Teen Numbers using Picture Graphs
- **Standards:** 1.MD.4
- **Prerequisites:** 1-4 ○ 1-34
- **Description:** Use picture graphs to compare categories and quantify their difference. Count extra and missing objects, figure out how many to add or subtract to make two categories equal, and subtract larger and smaller groups to find their difference. Explore and discover different ways of knowing how many more and how many fewer.

### 36. Quantify and Compare Teen Numbers using Ten Frames
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-32 ○ K-102
- **Description:** Use ten frames and "compare-the-parts" strategy to quantify the difference between 2 teen numbers. What's the difference between 13 and 11? Since both have a full ten frame, they balance and cancel each other out. Compare what's left. 3 is 2 more than 1, so 13 is 2 more than 11. The difference between 13 and 11 is 2.

### 37. Place Value to 20: Expanded Form
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** K-110 ○ 1-31
- **Description:** Lay the foundation for place value. Start by modeling teen numbers on ten frames. 12 is 10 counters and 2 counters – 10 ones and 2 ones – 12 ones in all. Transition to using Base 10 blocks or snap cubes to "unitize" 10 ones as 1 rod or unit. 12 is now 1 ten and 2 ones – 1 rod and 2 ones – not 12 cubes or counters.

### 38. Subitize Teen Numbers using Base 10 Blocks
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-37 ○ 1-31
- **Description:** Use quick images to build number sense and confidence with 2-digit numbers. Encourage subitizing instead of counting by limiting processing time. A "full" rod has a value of 10. Subitize what's left – what's there or what's missing – whichever is easier. 2 missing? There are 10+8 = 18 in all. 3 missing? 17 in all.

### 39. Quantify & Compare Numbers to 20 using Base 10 Blocks
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-36
- **Description:** Use Base 10 blocks to model expanded form, encourage place-value thinking, and quantify the difference between 2 teen numbers. How much greater is 16 than 12? Both have 1 ten, so compare what's left. 6 is 4 more than 2, so 16 is 4 more than 12. Comparing smaller parts is easier than comparing larger totals.

## 4. Add to 20

### 40. Bridge 10 by Counting On
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 1-3 ○ K-68
- **Description:** Use ten frames and the "counting-on" strategy as a bridge to the Make 10 Strategy. What's 8+5? Start with 8 on a ten frame. Count 2 more to make 10 – a full ten frame. Count 3 more to make 13. Notice the 5 is counted in 2 parts – a group of 2 and a group of 3. With practice, count on using a digit and ten frame, then 2 digits.

### 41. Add using the Make 10 with Left Strategy using Ten Frames
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-40 ○ K-74
- **Description:** Transition from counting to make 10, to adding to make 10. What's 8+6? Model both parts on ten frames. Shift 2 counters to the left ten frame to make 10. Notice the 6 is now in two parts – 2 and 4. Since 8+2 = 10 and 10+4 = 14, 8+6 = 14. Start by using a concrete model – real or drawn counters on ten frames.

### 42. Add using the Make 10 with Left Strategy using Number Bonds & Equations
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-41
- **Description:** Transition from modeling numbers with concrete ten frames, to modeling and breaking apart numbers more abstractly with pictorial number bonds. What's 8+3? Break 3 into 2 and 1. Since 8+2 = 10 and 10+1 = 11, 8+3 = 11. When ready, progress to mental math. Visualize the 7 as 1 and 6, without drawing the number bond.

### 43. Add using the Make 10 with Right Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 1-41
- **Description:** Think flexibly by making 10 with the second addend, instead of the first. What's 6+8? Think of 6 as 4 and 2. Make 10 by shifting 2 from the 6 to the 8. Since 8+2 = 10 and 10+4 = 14, 6+8 = 14. Start by using concrete models – real or drawn counters on ten frames. Transition to pictorial number bonds.

### 44. Add using the Make 10 with 5s Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 1-43 ○ K-67
- **Description:** When both addends are greater than 5, introduce a third Make 10 strategy. What's 9+8? Think of 9 as 5 and 4. Think of 8 as 5 and 3. Add 5+5 = 10, 4+3 = 7, and 10+7 = 17. Add the 2 partial sums to get the total sum. Prepare for double-digit addition by breaking both numbers apart. Add the 2 partial sums to get the total sum.

### 45. Master all Make 10 Addition Strategies
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-44, 1-43, 1-42
- **Description:** Practice using all 3 Make 10 strategies. Develop visualization and abstract thinking skills by using pictorial number bonds instead of ten frames. When both addends are greater than 5, any of the 3 strategies can be used. When only 1 addend is greater than 5, use either the Make 10 Left or Make 10 Right strategy.

### 46. Meaning of the Equal Sign
- **Standards:** 1.OA.7
- **Prerequisites:** 1-24 ○ 1-23, 1-22
- **Description:** Reinforce the meaning of the equal sign by comparing the value of one side of an equation with the value of the other side. Do both sides have the same value? If they do, both sides are equal and the equation is "true." If both sides are not equal, the equation is "false" and the "equation" is not really an equation.

### 47. Change & Start Unknown Addition to 20
- **Standards:** 1.OA.7, 1.OA.8
- **Prerequisites:** 1-18 ○ 1-45
- **Description:** Continue applying the all-important Make 10 strategy to solve challenging "change" and "start" unknown problems. Reinforce the meaning of the equal sign, and see the connection between subtracting and adding. Start with equations that have one expression. When ready, progress to equations with two expressions.

### 48. Compare Addition Expressions with Greater, Less, or Equal
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6, 1.NBT.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-26
- **Description:** Use the Make 10 strategy to simplify addition expressions. Compare values and show their relationship using a greater than (>), less than (<), or equal to (=) symbol. Start by comparing an expression and a number using ten frames. Progress to comparing 2 expressions. For a challenge, compare using mental math.

### 49. Add within 20 using the Place Value Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 1-16 ○ 1-37
- **Description:** Adding is easier when the there's no regrouping. What's 14+2? Model both numbers on ten frames. Since 4+2 = 6, 14+2 = 10+6 = 16. What's 13+4? 13 is 10 and 3 more. Since 3+4 = 7, 13+4 = 10+7 = 17. When ready, transition to using number bonds instead of ten frames, and finally, use only mental math.

### 50. Add within 20 using the Doubles Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 1-44
- **Description:** Learn new doubles facts by scaffolding from known, smaller facts. What's 6+6? Use 2 ten frames to see its 5+5 + 1+1. Since 5+5 = 10 and 1+1 = 2, 6+6 = 10+2 = 12. What's 7+7? It's 5+5 + 2+2 = 10+4 = 14. When ready, transition from concrete ten frames to using pictorial number bonds, and finally, just mental math.

### 51. Add within 20 with 3 Addends
- **Standards:** 1.OA.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-45
- **Description:** Generalize from adding 2 numbers – called addends – to adding 3 numbers. What's 9+1+3? Think strategically by making 10 first. 9+1 = 10 and 10+3 = 13. Use the associative property to add any 2 addends, then add the third.

### 52. Model Number vs Size of Equal Groups with Snap Cubes
- **Standards:** 1.OA.5
- **Description:** Use snap cubes to model equal groups concretely. Since there are no gaps or overlaps, the length of each bar equals the number of cubes. Encourage students to see the difference between the number of equal groups, and the number in each group – the size of each group. Find the total by adding or counting in smart ways.

### 53. Model Equal Groups using Discrete Circles in 3x5 Arrays
- **Standards:** 1.OA.5
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-52
- **Description:** Draw and make sense of arrays with up to 3 rows and 5 columns. Write an addition equation that represents the total number of circles. Follow the convention that the number of rows is the number of equal groups and the number of columns is the size of each group. Add or count in smart ways to figure out how many in all.

### 54. Model Equal Groups using Contiguous Squares in 3x5 Arrays
- **Standards:** 1.OA.5
- **Prerequisites:** 1-53
- **Description:** Draw and make sense of arrays with up to 3 rows and 5 columns of unit squares with no gaps or overlaps. Write an addition equation for the total number of unit squares. Use the number of rows for the number of groups and the number of columns for the size of each group. Add, group, or count to find how many in all.

### 55. Count Forward & Backward within 20 using Sets & Digits
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.1
- **Prerequisites:** K-66, K-65
- **Description:** Count forward & backward by putting arrays and "near arrays" in order. Use grouping strategies to figure out how many are in each set. When counting forward, understand that each number counted represents a set with 1 more shape. 13 is greater than 12. How much greater? 13 shapes are 1 more than 12 shapes.

## 5. Subtract to 20

### 56. Subtract within 20 using the Place Value Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** K-96 ○ 1-37
- **Description:** Use a compare-the-parts, place-value strategy when the difference is 10 or greater. What's 17-4? Use a ten frame to model 17 in expanded form – 10 and 7. Since 7-4 = 3, 17-4 = 10+3 = 13. What's 13-2? Model 13 as 10 and 3. Since 3-2 = 1, 13-2 = 10+1 = 11. When ready, progress to number bonds and mental math.

### 57. Subtract using the Make 10 Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-42, 1-41 ○ 1-37, K-109
- **Description:** Use the Make 10 Strategy to subtract a single-digit number. What's 13-5? Visualize 13 on ten frames. Subtract 5 in 2 parts. Imagine crossing off 3 to make 10, then crossing off 2 more. How many are left? 8. Check for accuracy by using or drawing counters. Since 13-3 = 10 and 10-2 = 8, 13-5 = 8.

### 58. Subtract using the From 10 Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** K-96 ○ 1-57
- **Description:** Instead of the Make 10 strategy, now use the From 10 Strategy. What's 17-9? Visualize 17 on 2 ten frames as 10 and 7. Imagine subtracting 9 from the 10. How many are left? There is 1 left on that ten frame, and 7 left on the other. There are 8 left in all. Check by using or drawing counters. Since 10-9 = 1 and 1+7 = 8, 17-9 = 8.

### 59. Subtract using the Missing Part Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.OA.4, 1.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** K-86 ○ 1-36
- **Description:** A third strategy is to think of subtraction as a "missing part" problem. What's 13-9? What's the difference between the total 13 and one of its parts, 9? Use ten frames to model 13, the total. Imagine 9, the known part. What's the other part? 9 + __ = 13? Need 1 to make 10 and 3 more to make 13, so the missing part is 1+3 = 4.

### 60. Master all Subtract within 20 Strategies
- **Standards:** 1.OA.4, 1.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 1-59, 1-58, 1-57, 1-56
- **Description:** Given the total and one of the parts, use the Make 10, From 10, or Missing Part strategy to find the other, unknown part. Use pictorial number bonds to model and solve problems more abstractly. For a challenge and to check for accuracy, solve problems using 2 – or even all 3 – strategies.

### 61. Meaning of Equal Sign
- **Standards:** 1.OA.7
- **Prerequisites:** 1-46 ○ 1-60
- **Description:** Reinforce the meaning of the equal sign while practicing the all-important Make 10 subtraction strategy. Compare the value of one side of an equation to the value of the other side. Are both sides equal? Start with equations that have one expression. When ready, progress to equations that have two equations.

### 62. Change & Start Unknown Subtraction within 20
- **Standards:** 1.OA.7, 1.OA.8
- **Prerequisites:** 1-21 ○ 1-57
- **Description:** Continue applying the all-important Make 10 strategy to solve challenging "change" and "start" unknown problems. Reinforce the meaning of the equal sign, and see the connection between subtracting and adding. Start with equations that have one expression. When ready, progress to equations with two expressions.

### 63. Compare Subtraction Expressions with Greater, Less, or Equal
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.3, 1.OA.7

## 7. Fraction Foundations

### 77. Identify Equal Lengths & Equal Parts of Bars
- **Standards:** 1.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** K-131 ○ K-71
- **Description:** Lay the foundation for fractions by identifying bars with equal lengths. Start by stacking and aligning 3 bars to make comparing easier. Which 2 have the same length? Which bar is the longest? Which is the shortest? Transition to bars that are side-by-side – parts of a whole bar – and not as easy to compare, visually.

### 78. Name Halves, Thirds & Fourths
- **Standards:** 1.G.3, 1.MD.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-77 ○ K-132
- **Description:** Divide a bar – the whole – into 2, 3, or 4 equal parts. Learn their names – 1 half, 1 third, and 1 fourth. How many make the whole? 2 halves, 3 thirds, and 4 fourths. Model the part-whole relationship concretely with real and virtual bars, and pictorially with pictures of bars and by drawing bars.

### 79. Model Halves, Thirds & Fourths with Bars
- **Standards:** 1.G.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-78 ○ K-133
- **Description:** Start with 1-dimensional, linear models. Divide the length of a bar into 2, 3, or 4 equal parts. To draw 2 halves or 3 thirds, estimate visually. To draw 4 fourths, first draw 2 halves. Divide each half in half to make 4 equal parts.

### 80. Model Halves, Thirds & Fourths with Squares
- **Standards:** 1.G.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-79 ○ K-134
- **Description:** Generalize from dividing the length of a bar to dividing the area or space "inside" a square into equal parts. Explore different ways of drawing 2 halves, 3 thirds, and 4 fourths – using horizontal, vertical, and diagonal lines. Fold paper to model and reinforce equal parts concretely.

### 81. Model Halves, Thirds & Fourths with Circles
- **Standards:** 1.G.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-80
- **Description:** Divide circles into equal parts by first drawing a dot in the center. Draw a line thru the dot to draw 2 halves. Draw 3 lines from the edge of the circle to the center to draw 3 thirds. To draw 4 fourths, draw 2 halves and divide each half in half. To model fractional parts of a circle concretely, use a cookie, pizza, or paper plate.

### 82. Model Unit Fractions with Bars
- **Standards:** 1.G.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-79
- **Description:** Partition the length of a bar into 2, 3, or 4 equal parts. See and understand halves, thirds, and fourths as equal, countable parts in a whole. Color 1 part. 1 out of 2 equal parts is 1 half of the whole. 1 out of 3 equal parts is 1 third. 1 out of 4 equal parts is 1 fourth or 1 quarter. Note that any of the equal parts can be colored.

### 83. Model Unit Fractions with Squares
- **Standards:** 1.G.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-82 ○ 1-80
- **Description:** Generalize from the length of a bar to the space or area inside a square. Draw and divide a square into 2, 3, or 4 equal parts. Reinforce understanding of unit fractions by coloring any 1 of the parts to show 1 half, 1 third, or 1 fourth. When the square or whole is the same, 1 half > 1 third, 1 half > 1 fourth, and 1 third > 1 fourth.

### 84. Model Unit Fractions with Circles
- **Standards:** 1.G.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-83 ○ 1-81
- **Description:** Generalize from squares to circles. Reinforce understanding of unit fractions by identifying and drawing 1 half, 1 third, and 1 fourth. Start by drawing a circle with a dot in the center. Partition the circle into 2, 3, or 4 equal parts. Draw fourths by dividing halves in half. Color 1 of the equal parts to represent a unit fraction.

### 85. Compare Unit Fractions
- **Standards:** 1.MD.2
- **Prerequisites:** 1-82
- **Description:** Compare the unit fractions 1 half, 1 third, and 1 fourth. When the whole is the same, more equal parts mean smaller parts. Fewer equal parts mean larger parts. There is an inverse relationship between the number of pieces and size of equal pieces. Length models – colored rods and bars – make comparing fractions, easier.

### 86. Match Unit Fractions
- **Standards:** 1.G.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-85 ○ K-134
- **Description:** Use conventional and unconventional representations of unit fractions to enrich, assess, and deepen understanding. Vary the part that's shaded, vary the whole to include equilateral triangles and rotated squares, and lay the foundation for fraction notation by writing "1 half," "1 third," and "1 fourth" – vertically.

### 87. Quantify Numbers to 50 using Ten Frames
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-37 ○ K-114, K-113
- **Description:** Use vertical ten frames to model numbers 20 to 50. What's 2 full ten frames and 4 more? 2 tens + 4 ones = 20+4 = 24. How many more make 30? 6 more make 30, since 24+6 = 30. ### 89. Subitize Numbers to 50 using Ten Frames
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.1

### 91. Compare Numbers to 50 using Base 10 Blocks*

## 10. Operations to 100

### 108. Add a Single-Digit Number with No Regrouping
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.4
- **Prerequisites:** 1-49
- **Description:** Add 2-digit and 1-digit numbers with no regrouping. First add the ones, then add back the tens to get the total. What's 26+2? Since 6+2 = 8 and 20+8 = 28, 26+2 = 28. ### 109. Add using the Make 20 Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.4

- **Description:** Generalize the Make 10 strategy to the Make 20 strategy. Continue using ten frames to model the parts and total. What's 18 + 6? Break 6 into 2 parts. ### 110. Add using the Make 10s Strategy with Base 10 Blocks
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.4
- **Prerequisites:** 1-45
- **Description:** Generalize from making 20 to making the next multiple of 10. Use Base 10 blocks or snap cubes instead of ten frames. What is 25 + 8? Break 8 into 2 parts. First make 30, then add the rest. 8 = 5 + 3, so add 25 + 5 = 30, then add 30 + 3 = 33. Think in groups of 10.

### 111. Add using the Make 10s Strategy with Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.4
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-110
- **Description:** Transition from concrete Base 10 blocks to pictorial number bonds. What's 56+6? Think of 6 as 4 and 2. Since 56+4 = 60 and 60+2 = 62, 56+6 = 62. - **Prerequisites:** K-117
- **Description:** Model multiples of 10 concretely with Base 10 blocks and discs. At the same time, lay the foundation for place value by writing equations and identifying tens and ones. ### 128. U.S. Money - Identify & Compare Coins*
- **Standards:** (none listed)
- **Prerequisites:** K-120 ○ K-119
- **Description:** Enrich by learning the names and attributes of coins. Learn and compare their value in cents (¢) by comparing their size. For quarters, nickels, and pennies, larger size means greater value. Dimes are the exception. They are smaller in size than pennies and nickels, but greater in value.

### 129. U.S. Money - Add & Compare Groups of Coins*
- **Standards:** (none listed)
- **Prerequisites:** 1-128 ○ 1-117, 1-112, 1-108
- **Description:** Enrich and reinforce the value of pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters by finding the total value of 2 coins. Compare groups using the greater than (>), less than (<), or equal (=) symbol.

### 130. Tell Time - Learn the Hour Hand
- **Standards:** 1.MD.3
- **Description:** Learn to read time on an analog clock. Learn to write time using digital notation. Start with a clock that has only the shorter, hour hand. The hour hand shows the number of hours after 12 o'clock – written as 12:00. What time is it when the hour hand points directly at 4? It is 4 hours after 12:00, so the time is 4:00.

### 131. Tell Time - Learn the Minute Hand
- **Standards:** 1.MD.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-130
- **Description:** Read a clock with just the longer, minute hand. The digits 1 to 12 still represent hours, so connect each hour with minutes. 12 is 0 min, 3 is 15 min, 6 is 30 min, and 9 is 45 min. If the minute hand points at 12, it's 0 min after the hour. If it points at 6, it's 30 min after the hour – 1 half hour. If it points at 2, it's 10 min after the hour.

### 132. Tell Time - Whole Hours & Half-Hours
- **Standards:** 1.MD.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-131, 1-130
- **Description:** Put hours and minutes together by reading a clock that has both hands. What time is it if the hour hand points at 2 and the minute hand points at 12? It's 2:00 – 2 hours and 0 minutes after 12:00. What time is it if the hour hand points at 1 and the minute hand points at 6? It's 1:30 – 1 hour and 30 minutes after 12:00, – or "half past 1."

### 133. Tell Time - Morning, Noon, and Night
- **Standards:** 1.MD.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-132
- **Description:** Use context to make sense of clock times by matching events to times in the morning, afternoon, and night. When do people typically eat breakfast – 11:00 at night, 7:00 in the morning, or 2:30 in the afternoon? Use reasoning and the process of elimination to connect and understand time to the nearest whole and half hour.

### 134. Defining Attributes of 2D Shapes
- **Standards:** 1.G.1
- **Prerequisites:** K-123
- **Description:** Given a flat shape, count how many straight sides, and how many corners or vertices it has. Based on its defining attributes, identify and match shapes to their names – circle, triangle, square, rectangle, and trapezoid. Learn to draw shapes, first by tracing and then by connecting dots with straight or circular lines.

### 135. Compose & Decompose 2D Shapes
- **Standards:** 1.G.2
- **Prerequisites:** 1-134
- **Description:** Apply part-whole thinking to shapes. Identify smaller shapes or parts that make or compose a larger whole. Start by identifying shapes, then progress to drawing composite shapes made of circles, triangles, squares, rectangles, hexagons and trapezoids.

## Overview

First-grade math builds directly on Kindergarten foundations, with focus on four critical areas: developing understanding of addition and subtraction (and related strategies) within 20; exploring whole-number relationships and place value (especially grouping in tens and ones); understanding linear measurement as iterating length units; and reasoning about attributes of, and composing/decomposing, geometric shapes. Students represent and solve word problems involving addition and subtraction, learn properties like the commutative and associative laws, and become fluent with facts within 20. They extend the counting sequence, understand that two-digit numbers are composed of tens and ones, and use place-value strategies to add or subtract within 100. In measurement and data, students measure lengths with nonstandard and standard units, compare lengths, tell and write time in hours and half-hours, and work with money (identifying coins and their values). They organize, represent, and interpret data with simple graphs. Geometry deepens as students distinguish defining attributes (e.g., number of sides or corners) from non-defining ones, compose two-dimensional shapes to create new ones, and partition shapes into equal shares while describing the parts. Hands-on activities and visual models help students connect these concepts, fostering both procedural fluency and conceptual understanding.
