## 1. Tune Up

### 1. Place Value - Shift 100s & 10s
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-2
- **Description:** Use Base Ten discs and number bonds to model quantities, place values, and expanded form. Prepare for subtraction with regrouping by shifting 1 hundred to the tens place and 1 ten to the ones place. Start by shifting discs in 2 steps. Transition to using number bonds. The goal is to shift 100 and shift 10 in one step.

### 2. Add to 1,000 with 1 Regrouping
- **Standards:** 3.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-10 ○ 2-95
- **Description:** Use Base 10 discs to model and visualize 3-digit addition with 1 regrouping. What's 324+418? Use a partial sums strategy to add hundreds, tens, and ones. 300+400 = 700, 20+10 = 30, and 4+8 = 12. Shift 10 from 12 to 30 to get 324+418 = 700+40+2 = 742. Transition from Base 10 discs to pictorial number bonds.

### 3. Add to 1,000 with 2 Regroupings
- **Standards:** 3.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-2
- **Description:** Progress from regrouping once to regrouping twice. What's 173+358? Use partial sums to add hundreds, tens, and ones. 100+300 = 400, 70+50 = 120, and 3+8 = 11. Shift 100 from 120 to 400, and shift 10 from 11 to the 20 that's left. 173+358 = 500+30+1 = 531. Start with Base 10 discs and transition to number bonds.

### 4. Subtract to 1,000 with 1 Regrouping
- **Standards:** 3.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-1 ○ 2-106
- **Description:** Use Base 10 discs to model and visualize 3-digit subtraction with 1 regrouping. What's 543-216? Use a partial differences strategy. Check the hundreds, tens, and ones. There are not enough ones, so shift 10. Think of 543 as 500, 30, and 13. 500-200 = 300, 30-10 = 20, and 13-6 = 7, so 543-216 = 300+20+7 = 327.

### 5. Subtract to 1,000 with 2 Regroupings
- **Standards:** 3.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-4
- **Description:** Progress from regrouping once to regrouping twice. What's 481-196? Check the hundreds, tens, and ones. There are not enough tens or ones, so shift 100 and 10. Think of 481 as 300, 170, and 11. 300-100 = 200, 170-90 = 80, and 11-6 = 5, so 481-196 = 200+80+5 = 285. Start with Base 10 discs. Transition to number bonds.

### 6. Multiply to 100 with Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-39 ○ 3-31
- **Description:** Use bar models to visualize equal groups. For 2 to 5 groups, draw each group explicitly. For 6 to 9 groups, draw the first 2 groups, draw an ellipsis, then draw the last group. Use known facts to find partial products, and add partial products to get total products. 6 is 3 and 3, 7 is 5 and 2, 8 is 4 and 4, and 9 is 5 and 4.

### 7. Multiply to 100 with Area Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.1, 3.MD.6
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-6 ○ 3-107, 3-89
- **Description:** Supplement bar models with arrays and area models. Start with arrays that show equal groups as rows of unit squares. Group rows to find partial products. Add partial products to get the total product. Progress to area models without unit squares. Multiply side lengths to get partial areas. Add partial areas to get the total area.

### 8. Multiply to 100 with Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4, 3.OA.5
- **Prerequisites:** 4-7 ○ 3-108, 3-92
- **Description:** Use number bonds to multiply by breaking the number of groups into smaller, more manageable parts. Apply the distributive property to find and add partial products to get the total product. What's 6x9? Think of 6 as 3 and 3. 6x9 = 3x9 + 3x9 = 27+27 = 54. Think proportionally and use known facts to master new facts.

### 9. Multiply to 100 Fluently
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4, 3.OA.5
- **Prerequisites:** 4-8
- **Description:** Extend the basic times tables to multiply single and double-digit numbers. Break factors into expanded form. Find and add partial products to get the total product. What's 3x12? Since 12 = 10+2, 3x12 = 3x10 + 3x2 = 30+6 = 36. Start with area models and transition to using number bonds and, with practice, mental math.

### 10. Multiply to 100 Story Problems
- **Standards:** 3.OA.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-113 ○ 4-9, 3-34
- **Description:** Use story problems to provide context for multiplication. Given the number of equal groups and the size of each group, apply the distributive property to find and add partial products to get the total product. Use bar models to visualize the relationship between the parts and total.

### 11. Divide to 100 with Bar Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2, 3.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 3-68
- **Description:** Use bar models to divide by visualizing the relationship between the number of groups, size of each group, and total. What's 12÷2? For the sharing model, divide the total into 2 equal groups. Find how many in each group. For the grouping model, divide the total into groups of 2. Find the number of equal groups.

### 12. Divide to 100 with Area Models
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4, 3.OA.6
- **Prerequisites:** 3-101 ○ 3-96, 3-93
- **Description:** Use area models to divide. What's 35÷5? If a rectangle's area is 35 and the length of one side is 5, what's the length of the other side? One strategy is to think of the total area 35 as 25 and 10. Since 5x5 = 25 and 5x2 = 10, the missing partial side lengths are 5 and 2. The missing total side length is 5+2 = 7, so 35÷5 = 7.

### 13. Divide to 100 Fluently
- **Standards:** 3.OA.4, 3.OA.5
- **Prerequisites:** 4-12 ○ 3-110
- **Description:** Use number bonds to divide by breaking the dividend (total) into smaller, easier-to-divide parts. What's 48÷6? One strategy is to think of 48 as 24+24. 48÷6 = (24+24) ÷ 6 = (24÷6) + (24÷6) = 4+4 = 8. Instead of dividing in one step, apply the distributive property. Find and add partial quotients to get the total quotient.

### 14. Divide to 100 with 2-Digit Quotients
- **Standards:** 3.OA.2, 3.OA.4
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-13 ○ 3-111
- **Description:** Use Base 10 discs and number bonds to divide with 2-digit quotients. Start with problems that don't require regrouping. What's 84÷2? 84 = 80+4, so 84÷2 = 80÷2 + 4÷2 = 40+2 = 42. Progress to regrouping problems. What's 81÷3? 81 = 80+1, but neither divides by 3. Shift 20 to get 60+21. 81÷3 = 60÷3 + 21÷3 = 20+7 = 27.

### 15. Divide to 100 Story Problems
- **Standards:** 3.OA.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-68 ○ 3-62
- **Description:** Use story problems to provide context for division. Given the total and number of equal groups, use the sharing model of division to find how many in each group. Divide using number bonds or the partial quotients algorithm. Draw bar models to visualize the relationship between the parts and total.

## 2. Multiplicative Comparison

### 16. Compare Numbers and find the Scale Factor by Multiplying
- **Standards:** 4.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-9
- **Description:** Introduce multiplicative comparison. How much greater is 24 than 8? Instead of thinking additively that 24 is 16 more than 8, think multiplicatively and find the scale factor – 24 is how many times 8. Draw a bar model or use multiplication facts to know 3x8 = 24, so 3 is the scale factor and 24 is 3 times as many as 8.

### 17. Compare Numbers and find the Scale Factor by Dividing
- **Standards:** 4.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 4-16 ○ 4-13
- **Description:** To find large scale factors, divide instead of multiply. 78 is how many times 6? Use the grouping model and partial quotients to find how many 6s are in 78. 78 = 60+18, so 78÷6 = (60+18) ÷ 6 = (60÷6) + (18÷6) = 10+3 = 13. There are 13 groups of 6 in 78, so 13x6 = 78, 13 is the scale factor, and 78 is 13 times as many as 6.

### 18. Multiplicative Comparison Story Problems: Scale-Factor-Unknown
- **Standards:** 4.OA.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-17
- **Description:** Use story problems to provide context for comparing 1 and 2-digit numbers, multiplicatively. Use the sharing model of division to calculate the scale factor – the larger number is how many times the smaller number. Use bar models and the distributive property to find and add partial quotients to get the total quotient.

### 19. Multiplicative Comparison Story Problems: Larger-Unknown & Smaller-Unknown
- **Standards:** 4.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-18
- **Description:** Solve multiplicative comparison problems where the scale factor and one number are known. If the smaller number is known, multiply to find the larger number. If the larger number is known, divide to find the smaller number. Use bar models to make sense of story problems that compare numbers multiplicatively.

### 20. Multiplicative Comparison Story Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-19, 4-18 ○ 3-10, 3-9
- **Description:** Assess understanding of multiplicative comparison by varying which is unknown – the scale factor, the larger number, or the smaller number. Multiply using partial products when the larger number is unknown. Divide using the grouping model and partial quotients when the scale factor or smaller number is unknown.

### 21. Multistep Comparison Story Problems: Scale-Factor-Unknown
- **Standards:** 4.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-20 ○ 4-18
- **Description:** Use bar models to solve problems that compare numbers both additively and multiplicatively. If 6 is the smaller number and 42 is the difference, what's the larger number and scale factor? Since the larger number is 6+42 = 48, the scale factor is 48÷6 = 8. 48 is 42 more than 6. 42 is also 8 times as many as 6.

### 22. Multistep Comparison Story Problems: Difference-Unknown
- **Standards:** 4.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-20 ○ 3-7
- **Description:** Transition to solving problems where the scale factor is known, but the difference is unknown. If the smaller number is 8 and the scale factor is 6, what's the larger number and the difference? Since the larger number is 6x8 = 48, the difference is 48-8 = 40. 48 is 6 times as many as 8. 48 is also 40 more than 8.

### 23. Multistep Comparison Story Problems: Total-Unknown*
- **Standards:** 4.OA.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-22 ○ 3-21
- **Description:** Provide enrichment by solving problems where the scale factor and difference are known, but both numbers and their sum are unknown. If the difference between 2 numbers is 36 and the scale factor is 5, what's their total? Draw a bar model to see their difference is 4u = 36, so 1u = 9 and their sum is 5u+1u = 6u = 6x9 = 54.

## 3. Place Value to 10,000

### 24. Place Value to Five Digits - Model and Compare
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-26
- **Description:** Use Base 10 discs to model expanded form. Identify every digit's quantity, value, and total value. What's 2,748? The place values from right to left are ones, tens, hundreds, and thousands, so 2,748 = (2 x 1,000) + (7 x 100) + (4 x 10) + (8 x 1) = 2,000 + 700 + 40 + 8. Start with discs, then transition to number bonds, then compare.

### 25. Renaming Numbers to 1,000
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** 3-22
- **Description:** Prepare for rounding and regrouping. Rename hundreds as tens, and hundreds and tens as ones. Use Base 10 blocks to see 1 hundred = 10 tens, 1 hundred = 100 ones, and 1 ten = 10 ones. How many tens are in 267? 2 hundreds = 20 tens, so 267 has 26 tens. How many ones? 267 = 200 ones + 60 ones + 7 ones = 267 ones.

### 26. Renaming Numbers to 10,000
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-25
- **Description:** Transition from renaming hundreds, to renaming thousands. How many hundreds in 7,155? 1 thousand = 10 hundreds, so 7 thousands = 70 hundreds. 7,155 has (70+1) hundreds = 71 hundreds. How many tens in 7,155? 1 thousand = 100 tens, so 7 thousands = 700 tens. There are (700+10+5) tens = 715 tens in all.

### 27. Rounding within 10,000 to the Nearest Hundred
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.3
- **Prerequisites:** 3-25 ○ 3-24
- **Description:** Round 4-digit numbers to the nearest hundred. Is 2,318 closer to 2,300 – the hundreds it has – or to 2,400 – one more hundred? Use an open number line to visualize both multiples of 100. Use a bar model to find 2,350 – the number halfway in-between. Since 2,318 < 2,350, 2,318 rounds down to 2,300.

### 28. Rounding within 10,000 to the Nearest Thousand
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.3
- **Prerequisites:** 4-27
- **Description:** Round 4-digit numbers to the nearest thousand. Is 2,659 closer to 2,000 – the thousands it has – or to 3,000 – one more thousand? Use an open number line to visualize both multiples of 1,000. Use a bar model to find 2,500 – the number halfway in-between. Since 2,659 > 2,500, it rounds up to 3,000.

### 29. Compare Digits to 10,000 Additively
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-24 ○ 4-5, 2-32, 2-12
- **Description:** Compare the value of a digit with the value of the same digit 1 place to the right. How much greater is the value of a 2 in the thousands place than a 2 in the hundreds place? Subtract to find the difference. 2,000-200 = 1,800. How much greater is the value of a 7 in the hundreds place than a 7 in the tens place? 700-70 = 630.

### 30. Compare Digits to 10,000 Multiplicatively
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.1, 4.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-29 ○ 3-109, 3-106
- **Description:** Transition to comparing multiplicatively a digit and the value of the same digit 1 place to the right. The value of a 3 in the thousands place is how many times the value of a 3 in the hundreds place? Find the scale factor by multiplying or dividing. 10 x 300 = 3,000 and 3,000÷300 = 10. 3,000 is 10 times the value of 300.

### 31. Compare Digits to 10,000 Additively & Multiplicatively
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.1, 4.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 4-30, 4-29
- **Description:** Compare both additively and multiplicatively, the value of a digit with the value of the same digit 1 place to the right. How much greater is the value of a 2 in the thousands place than a 2 in the hundreds place? It's 2,000–200 or 1,800 more. 2,000 is how many times 200? 10 times, since 2,000 ÷ 200 = 10, and 10x200 = 2,000.

### 32. Add to 10,000 using the Standard Algorithm
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.4
- **Prerequisites:** 4-3 ○ 3-3
- **Description:** Add vertically from right to left. Emphasize each digit's quantity and place value, or unit. What's 7,523 + 1,246? Since 3 ones + 6 ones = 9 ones, 2 tens + 4 tens = 6 tens, 5 hundreds + 2 hundreds = 7 hundreds, and 7 thousands + 1 thousand = 8 thousands, 7,523 + 1,246 = 8,000+700+60+9 = 8,769.

### 33. Subtract to 10,000 using the Standard Algorithm
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.4
- **Prerequisites:** 4-5 ○ 3-4
- **Description:** Subtract vertically from right to left. Solve no regrouping and regrouping problems. Emphasize each digit's quantity and place value, or unit. What's 8,678-4,136? Since 8 ones-6 ones = 2 ones, 7 tens-3 tens = 4 tens, 6 hundreds-1 hundred = 5 hundreds, and 8 thousands-4 thousands = 4 thousands. 8,678-4,136 = 4,542.

## 4. Multiply to 10,000

### 34. Multiply 1-Digit x 3-Digit Multiples of 100
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 3-106
- **Description:** Multiply a 1-digit number and multiple of 100 by thinking in hundreds. What's 3 x 500? 500 is 5 hundreds, so 3 x 5 hundreds = 15 hundreds = 10 hundreds + 5 hundreds = 1,000+500 = 1,500. Think of "00" as hundreds. What's 4 x 600? 4 x 6 hundreds = 24 hundreds = 20 hundreds + 4 hundreds = 2,000 + 400 = 2,400.

### 35. Multiply 1-Digit x 3-Digit with Area Models
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.5
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-107 ○ 4-7
- **Description:** Use area models to visualize the distributive property and multiply. What's 2 x 168? Draw a rectangle with side lengths of 2 and 168. Since 168 = 100+60+8, the area of the rectangle is (2x100) + (2x60) + (2x8) = 200+120+16 = 336. Think expanded form, then find and add partial areas to get the total area.

### 36. Multiply 1-Digit x 3-Digit with Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 4-35 ○ 3-108
- **Description:** Transition to using number bonds to break 3-digit factors into expanded form. Apply the distributive property by adding partial products to get the total product. Lay the groundwork for both addition and multiplication standard algorithms by writing equations with digits that are vertically aligned according to their place values.

### 37. Multiply 1-Digit x 3-Digit with the Partial Products Algorithm
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 4-36
- **Description:** Transition from number bonds to using the more compact and abstract, partial products algorithm. What's 3 x 215? Since 215 = 200+10+5, 3x215 = (3x200) + (3x10) + (3x5) = 600+30+15 = 645. Mentally break numbers into expanded form, and multiply from left-to-right. Find and add partial products to get the total product.

### 38. Multiply 1-Digit x 3-Digit Story Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-37 ○ 4-10
- **Description:** Use story problems to provide context for multiplication. Start with stories that have 1 sentence on each line. Transition to stories in paragraph form. Use bar models to visualize the relationship between the number of groups, the size of each group, and the total. Multiply using number bonds or the partial products algorithm.

### 39. Multiply 1-Digit x 3-Digit Perimeter & Area Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.2, 4.MD.3
- **Prerequisites:** 3-116 ○ 4-37
- **Description:** Use multistep, geometric-measurement problems to develop computational fluency and reasoning skills. If the short side of a rectangle is 6 and the long side is 19 times the short side, what's the perimeter and area? The long side is 19x6 = 114, so the perimeter is (2x6) + (2x114) = 240. The area is 6x114 = 600+60+24 = 684.

### 40. Multiply 2-Digit x 2-Digit with Area Models
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.5
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-35 ○ 4-36
- **Description:** Transition from multiplying 1-digit and 3-digit numbers, to multiplying two, 2-digit numbers. What's 13x24? Draw a rectangle with side lengths of 13 and 24. Break both sides into their expanded form. Find and add the four partial areas to get the total area. 13x24 = (10x20)+(10x4)+(3x20)+(3x4) = 200+40+60+12 = 312.

### 41. Multiply 2-Digit x 2-Digit with Area Models & Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 4-40 ○ 4-37
- **Description:** Transition from multiplying concretely with area models, to multiplying pictorially with number bonds. Solve the same problem both ways to see the underlying concept – the distributive property – is the same. Use mental math to find the 4 partial products. Add using the standard algorithm to find the total product.

### 42. Multiply 2-Digit x 2-Digit with Number Bonds & Partial Products Algorithm
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 4-10 ○ 4-37
- **Description:** Transition from number bonds to the more compact and abstract, partial products algorithm. What's 34 x 65? Since 34 = 30+4 and 65 = 60+5, apply the distributive property to get 34 x 65 = (30x60) + (30x5) + (4x60) + (4x5) = 1,800+150+240+20 = 2,210. Multiply from left-to-right. Add partial products to get the total product.

### 43. Multiply 2-Digit x 2-Digit Equal-Groups Story Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.3
- **Prerequisites:** 4-10 ○ 4-42
- **Description:** Use story problems to provide context for multiplying. If there are 18 rows with 65 in each row, how many in all? Is the product 18 x 65 or 65 x 18? It's both, but draw a bar models to see there are 18 groups of 65, so 18 x 65 is preferred. Lay the groundwork for multiplying fractions by emphasizing the "groups of" convention.

### 44. Multiply 2-Digit x 2-Digit Comparison Story Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-20 ○ 4-42
- **Description:** Use multiplication to compare 2 numbers multiplicatively instead of additively. If the smaller number is 14 and the larger number is 17 times as many, what's the larger number? Multiply 17 x 14 to find the larger number is 238. Use comparison story problems to develop multiplicative thinking and provide context for multiplying.

### 45. Multiply 2-Digit x 2-Digit Perimeter & Area Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.2, 4.MD.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 3-115 ○ 4-39
- **Description:** Use multistep geometric-measurement problems to develop computational fluency and reasoning skills. If the short side of a rectangle is 11, and the long side is 9 times the short side, what's the perimeter and area? The long side is 9x11 = 99, so the perimeter is (2x11) + (2x99) = 220. The area is 11x99 = 900+90+90+9 = 1,089.

## 5. Divide to 10,000

### 46. Divide Multiples of 100
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.6
- **Prerequisites:** 3-109
- **Description:** Divide a multiple of 100 by a single-digit number by thinking in units of 100. What's 4,200 ÷ 7? 4,000 = 40 hundreds and 200 = 2 hundreds, so 4,200 = 42 hundreds. 42 hundreds ÷ 7 = 6 hundreds = 600. Think of "00" as hundreds. What's 2,400 ÷ 3? 2,400 = 24 hundreds, and 24 hundreds ÷ 3 = 8 hundreds = 800.

### 47. Divide 3-Digit Numbers with Area Models
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.6
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-12 ○ 4-12
- **Description:** Divide using area models. What's 276÷4? Draw a rectangle with area 276 and short side of 4. What's the other side? 4 x __ = 276? Break the total area into partial areas. 276 = 240+36, so 276÷4 = (240÷4) + (36÷4). Since 4x60 = 240 and 4x9 = 36, 276÷4 = 60+9 = 69. Find and add partial quotients to get the total quotient.

### 48. Divide 3-Digit Numbers with Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.6
- **Prerequisites:** 4-47 ○ 4-13
- **Description:** Transition from area models to number bonds. Divide by breaking the total into smaller, known multiples that are easier to divide. What's 278÷2? Think of 278 as 200+60+18, or 2 hundreds, 6 tens, and 18 ones. 278÷2 = (200÷2) + (60÷2) + (18÷2) = 100+30+9 = 139. Find and add partial quotients to get the total quotient.

### 49. Divide 4-Digit Numbers with Area Models & Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.6
- **Prerequisites:** 4-48, 4-47
- **Description:** Generalize from 3-digit to 4-digit numbers. Divide by breaking the dividend into known multiples. Use area models to visualize the total area as partial areas. Use number bonds to break the total into smaller parts. For both models, apply the distributive property. Find and add partial quotients to get the total quotient.

### 50. Divide 3-Digit & 4-Digit Numbers using the Partial Quotient Algorithm
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.6
- **Prerequisites:** 4-49
- **Description:** Transition from pictorial models to the vertical algorithm. What's 561÷3? Apply the distributive property to make large numbers easier to divide. First, divide 300 by 3 to get 100. Next, divide 240 by 3 to get 80. Finally, divide 21 by 3 to get 7. Add the partial quotients to get the total quotient. 561÷3 = 100+80+7 = 187.

### 51. Divide-by-Sharing Story Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-15 ○ 3-114, 3-58
- **Description:** Use word problems to provide context for division. Given the total and number of equal groups, use the sharing model of division to find how many in each group. Divide using number bonds or the partial quotients algorithm. Draw bar models to visualize the relationship between the equal groups and the total.

### 52. Divide-by-Grouping Story Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-15 ○ 4-50, 3-114, 3-70
- **Description:** Transition to problems that provide context for division by grouping, rather than sharing. Given the total and size of each group, find the number of equal groups. Divide using number bonds or the partial quotients algorithm. Draw bar models to visualize the relationship between the equal groups and the total.

### 53. Division Measurement Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.3, 4.MD.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-52, 4-51
- **Description:** Use length to divide by sharing and grouping, while laying the foundation for geometric measurement. If the total length of the objects and the number of objects is given, find the length of each object. If the length of each object is given, find the number of objects. Convert larger to smaller units of measure.

### 54. Division 1-Step Perimeter Problems
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.6, 4.MD.3
- **Prerequisites:** 4-51, 4-50 ○ 3-64
- **Description:** Use perimeter to develop fluency with the partial quotients algorithm. Draw a bar model to represent the perimeter of a regular polygon – a shape with equal sides and angles. Use the sharing model to divide the perimeter by the number of sides. Find the length of each side, then multiply to check for accuracy.

### 55. Order of Operations with Grouping Symbols
- **Standards:** 4.NBT.5, 4.OA.3
- **Prerequisites:** 3-60
- **Description:** Solve equations using the correct order of operations. Simplify expressions inside parentheses first. Multiply and divide before adding and subtracting, otherwise left to right. What's 16x(12+13)? 12+13 = 25, and 16x25 = 400. Compare the answer 400 to the very common, but incorrect, answer, (16x12)+13 = 205.

### 56. Division 2-Step Perimeter & Area Problems
- **Standards:** 4.OA.3, 4.MD.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-54 ○ 3-117

- **Description:** Use Base 10 blocks to add tenths and hundredths. What's 8/10 + 1/10? If a flat is 1, a rod is 1/10. 8 rods + 1 rod = 9 rods, so 8/10 + 1/10 = 9/10. What's 29/100 + 11/100? Since a square is 1/100, 29 squares + 11 squares = 40 squares, and 29/100 + 11/100 = 40/100. Add fractions by thinking in units of tenths and hundredths.

### 114. Add Tenths & Hundredths by Renaming, using Base 10 Blocks
- **Standards:** 4.NF.5
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 4-112 ○ 3-22
- **Description:** Use Base 10 blocks to rename and add tenths to hundredths. What's 2/10 + 53/100? The denominators are not the same, so make hundredths – a common unit. If a flat is 1, each vertical column or rod is 1/10, and each square is 1/100. Since 2/10 is 2 columns, 20 squares, and 20/100, 2/10 + 53/100 = 20/100 + 53/100 = 73/100.

### 115. Write Fractions as Decimals
- **Standards:** 4.NF.6
- **Description:** Write decimal fractions using Base 10 notation. 3 tenths? It's .3, the digit 3 in the tenths place. 4 hundredths? It's .04, the digit 0 in the tenths place and the digit 4 in the hundredths place. 52 hundredths? Since 52/100 = 50/100 + 2/100 = 5/10 + 2/100, it's .52, the digit 5 in the tenths place and the digit 2 in the hundredths place.

### 116. Write Decimals as Fractions
- **Standards:** 4.NF.6
- **Prerequisites:** 4-115
- **Description:** Switch from decimal notation to fraction notation. What's .7? 7 in the tenths place, 7/10. What's .02? 0 in the tenths place, 2 in the hundredths place, 2/100. What's .30? 3 tenths and 0 hundredths are 30 hundredths and 0 hundredths, 30/100. What's .67? 6 tenths and 7 hundredths are 60 hundredths and 7 hundredths, 67/100.

### 117. Model Ones, Tenths & Hundredths using Base 10 Discs & Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 4.NF.6
- **Prerequisites:** 4-112 ○ 4-24
- **Description:** Use more abstract Base 10 discs and number bonds to model decimals and whole numbers. What's 1.24? Focus on each digit's quantity and place value. 1.24 = 1 one + 2 tenths + 4 hundredths = (1 x 1) + (2 x .1) + (4 x .01). To multiply decimals, multiply using fraction notation. (1 x 1) + (2 x 1/10) + (4 x 1/100) = 1 24/100 = 1.24.

### 118. Convert Ones into Tenths
- **Standards:** 4.NF.6
- **Prerequisites:** 4-117 ○ 3-22
- **Description:** Develop a better understanding of place value and decimals by thinking flexibly about ones and tenths. How many tenths in 2.5? One answer is 5, but 10 tenths = 1, so there are 20 more tenths in 2. There are 25 tenths in all. How many tenths in 4.3? 43 – the digits in the tenths and ones place. Explore and reason about why this is true.

### 119. Convert Ones & Tenths into Hundredths
- **Standards:** 4.NF.6
- **Prerequisites:** 4-118
- **Description:** Lay the foundation for regrouping with decimals. How many hundredths in 1.23? There's a 3 in the hundredths place, but also 10 hundredths in each tenth and 100 hundredths in 1. There are 123 hundredths in all. How many hundredths are in 2.75? 275 – the digits in the ones, tenths, and hundredths places. Reason about why this shortcut works.

### 120. Order Decimals on a Number Line
- **Standards:** 4.NF.6, 4.NF.7
- **Prerequisites:** 4-96
- **Description:** Build number sense with decimals by comparing decimals to each other and to benchmark numbers like .25, .5, .75, and 1.00. Which is greater, .13 or .5? Fraction notation can make comparing easier. Since .13 = 13/100 and .5 = 5/10 = 50/100, compare 13/100 and 50/100. 13/100 < 50/100 so .13 is less than .5.

### 121. Line Plots using Metric Units
- **Standards:** 4.MD.4
- **Prerequisites:** 4-120 ○ 4-106
- **Description:** Plot the frequency of length and weight data measured to the nearest tenth of a centimeter, meter, gram, or kilogram. Identify the minimum value, the maximum value, and the mode – the value observed most. Build number sense with decimals, and graphing skills with open number lines and line plots.

## 11. Measurement

### 122. Convert Units of Length
- **Standards:** 4.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 4-10
- **Description:** Draw bar models to visualize the relationship between larger and smaller units of length. 24 yards is how many feet? Start with the unit rate. Since 1 yd = 3 ft, 24 yd = 24 x 3 ft = 72 ft. Alternatively, 1 yd = 3 ft, so 4 yd = 12 ft, and 24 yd = 6 x 12 ft = 72 ft. Think proportionally and use partial products to multiply.

### 123. Convert Units of Weight & Mass
- **Standards:** 4.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 4-122
- **Description:** Generalize from converting units of length, to converting units of weight and mass. 18 pounds is how many ounces? Start with the unit rate. Since 1 lb = 16 oz, 18 lb = 18 x 16 oz = 288 oz. Alternatively, 1 lb = 16 oz, so 6 lb = 96 oz, and 18 lb = 3 x 96 oz = 288 oz. Think proportionally and use partial products to multiply.

### 124. Convert Units of Liquid Volume
- **Standards:** 4.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 4-123
- **Description:** Use unit rates to convert measurements of liquid volume from larger units to smaller units. Convert gallons to quarts, quarts to pints, pints to cups, and cups to fluid ounces. With metric units, convert liters to milliliters. Multiply by thinking proportionally and using partial products. Work to commit unit rates to memory.

### 125. Convert Units of Clock & Calendar Time
- **Standards:** 4.MD.1
- **Prerequisites:** 4-124
- **Description:** Use unit rates to convert lengths of time from larger to smaller units. 15 days is how many hours? 1 d = 24 hr, so 15 d = 15 x 24 hr = 360 hr. In 2 steps, 1 d = 24 hr, so 5 d = 120 hr, and 15 d = 3 x 120 hr = 360 hr. For any length of time, need more small units, or fewer large units. The number and size of units are inversely related.

### 126. Word Problems - Convert Units of Length, Weight & Liquid Volume
- **Standards:** 4.MD.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-124, 4-123, 4-122
- **Description:** Use bar models to convert a mix of measurements in larger units to smaller units. Provide unit rates to convert half gallons, quarts, and pints into fluid ounces, pounds into ounces, feet and yards into inches, and miles into yards and feet. Memorize common unit rates, and use proportional reasoning and partial products to multiply.

### 127. Word Problems - Convert Units of Calendar & Clock Time
- **Standards:** 4.MD.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-125
- **Description:** Use bar models to convert clock and calendar times in larger units to smaller units. Provide unit rates to convert years into months and weeks, fortnights into days, days into hours, hours into minutes, and minutes into seconds. Memorize common unit rates, and use proportional reasoning and partial products to multiply.

## 12. Geometry

### 128. Points, Line Segments, Rays & Lines
- **Standards:** 4.G.1
- **Description:** Identify and draw points, line segments, rays, and lines. A point is the smallest part of a line. A line segment connects 2 points. Its length is the distance between the points. A ray starts at a point and extends forever in 1 direction. A line extends forever in opposite directions. The length of a point, ray, or line can't be measured.

### 129. Draw, Describe & Classify Angles
- **Standards:** 4.MD.5
- **Prerequisites:** 4-128
- **Description:** Form angles using 2 rays that share a common endpoint. Describe and classify angles informally as "sharp" (acute), "wide" (obtuse), and "square" (right). Transition to formal names when students can measure angles in degrees with a protractor. Draw sharp, wide, and square angles, as well as figures that are not angles.

### 130. Measure & Classify Angles using a Protractor
- **Standards:** 4.MD.6
- **Prerequisites:** 4-129
- **Description:** Measure angles using a protractor. Classify by comparing angles to 90° right angles formed by perpendicular rays, and 180° straight angles formed by rays pointing in opposite directions. An angle less than 90° is an acute angle. An angle between 90° and 180° is an obtuse angle. A reflex angle is between 180° and 360°.

### 131. Add & Subtract Measurements of Angles
- **Standards:** 4.MD.7
- **Prerequisites:** 4-130 ○ 4-33, 4-32
- **Description:** Use part-whole thinking to add or subtract angles. If an angle measures 50° and an adjacent angle measures 20°, what do the 2 angles measure together? Angles are additive, so 50°+20° = 70°. If the sum of 2 adjacent angles is 90° and one angle is 30°, what's the other angle? Since 90°-30° = 60°, and 30°+60° = 90°, it's 60°.

### 132. Classify Lines - Intersecting, Perpendicular, Parallel
- **Standards:** 4.G.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-128
- **Description:** Classify lines as intersecting, perpendicular, or parallel. Lines that cross or eventually cross are intersecting. Intersecting lines that form 90° right angles are perpendicular. Lines that never intersect and stay the same distance apart forever are parallel. Use reasoning to classify and draw specific types of lines.

### 133. Classify Triangles - Acute, Obtuse, Right
- **Standards:** 4.G.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-129
- **Description:** Classify triangles based on the presence or absence of angles. Right triangles have 1 right angle (90°). Acute triangles have 3 acute angles (<90°). Obtuse triangles have 1 obtuse angle (>90°). Lay the groundwork for understanding hierarchy – of quadrilaterals and other shapes – by identifying shared attributes for 2 triangles.

### 134. Classify Quadrilaterals - Defining Attributes
- **Standards:** 4.G.2
- **Prerequisites:** 4-132
- **Description:** Classify quadrilaterals based on their defining attributes – the number of pairs of parallel sides, equal sides, perpendicular sides, and right angles. Start by identifying a shape's defining attributes. Progress to identifying all of its categories or shape names, as well. Lay the groundwork for understanding hierarchy.

### 135. Lines of Symmetry
- **Standards:** 4.G.3
- **Description:** Develop visualization skills by drawing and identifying lines of symmetry for circles, triangles, quadrilaterals, and regular polygons. A line of symmetry divides a shape into 2 equal parts. The parts must be mirror images of each other – same size, same shape, and lie on top of each other when the shape is folded along the line.

## Overview

Fourth-grade math emphasizes three critical areas: developing an understanding and fluency with multi-digit multiplication and division; understanding fraction equivalence, addition and subtraction of fractions with like denominators, and multiplication of fractions by whole numbers; and analyzing and classifying geometric figures based on properties. Students generalize place value to 1,000,000, use it to perform multi-digit arithmetic (including the standard algorithm for multiplication and division), and solve multistep word problems with whole numbers. They find factors and multiples, generate and analyze patterns, and convert measurements. Fraction work deepens dramatically: students recognize and generate equivalent fractions, compare fractions, add and subtract with like denominators, and multiply a fraction by a whole number. They extend understanding of decimals as fractions with denominators 10 or 100. Measurement and data include solving problems with distances, time, volumes, masses, and money, plus representing and interpreting data on line plots. Geometry focuses on drawing and identifying points, lines, rays, angles, and perpendicular/parallel lines; classifying two-dimensional figures by properties (parallel sides, angles, symmetry); and recognizing lines of symmetry. Students use visual models, equations, and reasoning to build deep conceptual understanding alongside procedural skill.
