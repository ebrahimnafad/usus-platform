## 1. Tune Up

### 1. Place Value to 20
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-37
- **Description:** Break numbers 10 to 20 into expanded form – 10 and some more. Use base 10 blocks, base 10 discs, and number bonds. What is 11? 10 and 1. 1 ten and 1 one. What is 12? 10 and 2. 1 ten and 2 ones. Use nicknames to emphasize units – tens and ones. 11 is "eleven" and "1 ten 1." 12 is "twelve" and "1 ten 2."

### 2. Place Value to 100
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** 1-105 ○ 1-99, 1-97, 1-95
- **Description:** Break numbers to 100 into expanded form – a multiple of 10 and some more. Use base 10 discs and number bonds. What is 23? 20 and 3. 2 tens and 3 ones. What is 34? 30 and 4. 3 tens and 4 ones. Use nicknames to emphasize units – tens and ones. 23 is "twenty-three" and "2 ten 3." 34 is "thirty-four" and "3 ten 4."

### 3. Subitize to 100
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-100 ○ 1-98, 1-96
- **Description:** Use quick images to develop number sense, build confidence, and lay the foundation for place-value. Start with ten frames. Progress to base 10 blocks and discs. Subitize how many in all, and how many more make the next multiple of 10 – the key to using the all-important Make 10s strategy to add.

### 4. Shift 10 with Base 10 Discs
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-118, 1-112 ○ 1-99, 1-97, K-117
- **Description:** Build number sense and prepare for regrouping by shifting 10 concretely with base 10 discs. What's 51? It's 50 and 1. Shift 1 ten. The total is still 51, but the two parts are now 40 and 11. The Funny Number "forty-eleven" is really 51. Learn by shifting 10 in 2 steps. With practice, visualize and mentally shift 10 in 1 step.

### 5. Shift 10 with Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.2
- **Prerequisites:** 2-4
- **Description:** Transition from using concrete base 10 discs to using pictorial number bonds. What's 34? It's 30 and 4. Shift 10. The new number bond is 20 and 14. The Funny Number "twenty-fourteen" equals the real number "thirty-four." Start by shifting 10 in 2 steps. With practice, visualize and shift 10 in 1 step.

### 6. Add using the Make 10 Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6, 2.OA.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-40 ○ 1-45, 1-43, 1-42, 1-41
- **Description:** Add single-digit numbers using the all-important Make 10 Addition Strategy. Start with concrete ten frames. What's 6+5? First make 10. Break 5 into 4 and 1. 6+4 = 10. 10+1 = 11. What's 7+8? Since 7+3 = 10, break 8 into 3 and 5. 7+3 = 10. 10+5 = 15. First make 10, then add the rest.

### 7. Add using the Make 20 Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.4
- **Prerequisites:** 2-6 ○ 1-109
- **Description:** Generalize from the Make 10 Strategy to the Make 20 Strategy. Use ten frames and number bonds to add a teen number and a single-digit number. What's 13+8? Need 7 to make 20. Break 8 into 7 and 1. 13+7 = 20. 20+1 = 21. What's 14+9? Break 9 into 6 and 3. 14+6 = 20. 20+3 = 23. First make 20, then add the rest.

### 8. Add using the Make 10s Strategy
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.4
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-7, 2-6 ○ 1-110, 1-45
- **Description:** Generalize from the Make 10 and Make 20 Strategy to the Make 10s Strategy. Use Base 10 blocks and number bonds to add a 2-digit and 1-digit number by making a multiple of ten. What's 26+7? Make 30. Break 7 into 4 and 3. 26+4 =30 and 30+3 = 33. What's 37+5? Make 40. Break 5 into 3 and 2. 37+3 = 40. 40+2 = 42.

### 9. Add 3 Single-Digit Numbers
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6, 2.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 1-51
- **Description:** Generalize from adding 2 numbers – called addends – to adding 3 numbers. What's 9+3+2? No pair makes 10, so add left to right. 9+3 = 12 and 12+2 = 14. What's 5+6+5? Think strategically by making 10 first. 5+5 = 10 and 10+6 = 16. Use the associative property to add any 2 addends, then add the third.

### 10. Subtract using the Make 10 Strategy with Ten Frames
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6, 2.OA.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-62 ○ 1-59, 1-58, 1-57
- **Description:** Use concrete ten frames and the all-important Make 10 strategy to subtract single-digit numbers. What's 12-5? First make 10. Think of 5 as 2 and 3. Since 12–2 = 10 and 10–3 = 7, 12-5 = 7. What's 13-4? Think of 4 as 3 and 1. Since 13-3 = 10 and 10-1 = 9, 13-4 = 9. Subtract to first make 10, then subtract the rest.

### 11. Subtract using the Make 10 Strategy with Number Bonds & Equations
- **Standards:** 1.OA.6, 2.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 2-10
- **Description:** Transition from ten frames to pictorial number bonds. Subtract single-digit numbers by first making 10, then subtracting the rest. What's 13-5? Think of 5 as 3 and 2. Since 13-3 = 10 and 10-2 = 8, 13-5 = 8. When ready, progress to using just equations. Visualize and subtract the parts using mental math.

### 12. Subtract Multiples of 10
- **Standards:** 1.NBT.6
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-118 ○ 1-112
- **Description:** Use base 10 discs to visualize and subtract multiples of 10. Use an equation at the same time that shows how many tens and ones there are to start, how many are taken away, and how many are left. What's 80-40? 80 is 8 tens. 40 is 4 tens. 8 tens - 4 tens leaves 4 tens, so 80-40 = 40. When ready, use only equations.

### 13. Add-To Result-Unknown Word Problems
- **Standards:** 1.OA.1, 2.OA.2
- **Prerequisites:** 1-64 ○ 1-9
- **Description:** Use story problems to provide context for addition. Given the starting quantity (start) and how many are added to it (change), find the total – how many in all (result). Draw a diagram to visualize the relationship between the parts and total. Add to find the unknown total.

### 14. Take-From Result-Unknown Word Problems
- **Standards:** 1.OA.1, 2.OA.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-13 ○ 1-69, 1-59, 1-58, 1-14
- **Description:** Use story problems to provide context for addition and subtraction. Given the starting quantity (start) and how many are taken away (change), find how many are left (result). Draw a diagram to visualize the relationship between the parts and total. Add or subtract to find the unknown part.

### 15. Compare Difference-Unknown Word Problems
- **Standards:** 1.OA.1, 2.OA.2
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-73 ○ 1-59, 1-58, 1-57
- **Description:** Use story problems to provide context for comparing. Draw a bar model (tape or strip diagram) to visually compare 2 quantities. Draw a dotted or "phantom" bar to represent how many more is the larger amount, and how many fewer is the smaller amount. Add or subtract to find the difference.

## 2. Grouping to 100

### 16. Even & Odd Numbers
- **Standards:** 2.OA.3
- **Prerequisites:** 1-33 ○ 1-117, 1-116
- **Description:** Model numbers with counters. If all the counters can be grouped in 2s, the number is even. If there's an extra counter leftover, the number is odd. Is the sum of 2 even numbers even or odd? The sum of 2 odd numbers? The sum of an even and an odd number? Develop reasoning skills by exploring even and odd numbers.

### 17. Model Equal Groups with Snap Cubes
- **Standards:** 2.OA.4
- **Description:** Use snap cubes to model equal groups. Represent values by the number and length of cubes snapped together. Focus on seeing, hearing, and understanding the difference between the number of groups and the number in each group – the size of each group. Add in smart ways to figure out how many in all – the total.

### 18. Model Equal Groups with Bar Models
- **Standards:** 2.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 2-17
- **Description:** Use bar models to model equal groups. Represent values by the lengths of bars. To draw equal groups, draw a shorter bar and make copies. Alternatively, draw a longer bar and divide it into equal lengths. Draw halves to make 2 equal groups. Divide each half in half to make 4 equal groups. Visually estimate 3 and 5 equal groups.

### 19. Model Arrays with Counters
- **Standards:** 2.OA.4
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-18
- **Description:** Color or draw an array with up to 5 rows and 5 columns of circles or dots. Use the convention that the number of rows represents the number of groups, and the number of columns represents the size or number in each group. An array with 4 rows and 2 columns is 4 groups of 2. Since 2+2+2+2 = 8, the array has 8 circles in all.

### 20. Model Arrays with Unit Squares
- **Standards:** 2.OA.4, 2.G.2
- **Prerequisites:** 2-19
- **Description:** Generalize from drawing arrays with circles and gaps to drawing arrays by partitioning rectangles into unit squares with no gaps. Draw 1 fewer horizontal line than the number of rows. Draw 1 fewer vertical line than the number of columns. How many squares in all? Add the number of squares in each row or column.

### 21. Model Arrays with Unit Cubes
- **Standards:** 2.OA.4
- **Prerequisites:** 2-20
- **Description:** Generalize from arrays in 2-dimensional space, to arrays in 3-dimensional space. Find the number of unit cubes that fit in the bottom layer of a rectangular prism – a box. Use grouping strategies to find the number of cubes in each row or column, and the total number of cubes in the base.

### 22. Find Patterns in Repeated Addition*
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.2, 3.OA.9
- **Prerequisites:** 2-19
- **Description:** Use patterns to build addition skills. Provide enrichment by laying the foundation for multiplication. Given a rule, find the first 6 numbers. Or given 4 numbers, find the rule. Generalize to find a number beyond the first 6 terms. Use reasoning to determine whether a number will be even or odd – without calculating the number.

### 23. Order of Operations*
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.6, 5.OA.1
- **Description:** Provide enrichment by introducing order of operations with addition and subtraction. Correct the common misconception that adding always comes before subtracting. Read equations left to right and do the operation that comes first. What's 8-2+4? Subtract first, then add. Since 8-2 = 6 and 6+4 = 10, 8-2+4 = 10.

### 24. Order of Operations with Parentheses*
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.6, 5.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-23
- **Description:** Provide enrichment by using parentheses - a grouping symbol - to group numbers and operations explicitly. Simplify expressions inside any parentheses first. What's 9-(5-2)? First subtract 5-2 = 3. Then subtract 9-3 = 6. What's (9-2) - (3+1)? First subtract 9-2 = 7. Then add 3+1 = 4. Finally, subtract 7-4 = 3.

## 3. Operations to 100

### 25. Add Double-Digit Numbers with No Regrouping
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-115 ○ 1-8
- **Description:** Add 2-digit numbers with Base 10 discs. What's 32+54? Break both numbers into expanded form. 32 is 30 and 2. 54 is 50 and 4. Since 30+50 = 80, 2+4 = 6, and 80+6 = 86, 32+54 = 86. Add the tens, add the ones, then add the partial sums to get the total sum. Progress to using number bonds, and finally, just equations.

### 26. Add Double-Digit Numbers with Regrouping
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 2-25 ○ 1-45
- **Description:** Transition to 2-digit numbers with more ones. What's 59+37? Since 50+30 = 80, 9+7 = 16, and 80+16 = 96, 59+37 = 96. Use a Make 10 strategy to add 9+7. Add 80+16 by breaking 16 into 10 and 6 and adding 80 + 10 + 6. Regrouping problems require stronger pre-requisite skills, but not more steps.

### 27. Add 3 Double-Digit Numbers
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.6
- **Prerequisites:** 2-26
- **Description:** Generalize from adding 2 addends to adding 3 addends. What's 23+12+19? Since 20+10+10 = 40, 3+2+9 = 14, and 40+14 = 54, 23+12+19 = 54. Find and add partial sums to get the total sum. Model first with concrete Base 10 discs, then progress to using pictorial number bonds and abstract equations.

### 28. Add 3 Double-Digit Equal Groups
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.6
- **Prerequisites:** 2-27
- **Description:** Generalize from adding unequal to equal groups. Encourage Base 10 thinking by adding tens and then ones. What's 12+12+12? 10+10+10 = 30, 2+2+2 = 6, and 30+6 = 36. 3 groups of 12 is 36. Model concretely with Base 10 discs. Progress to number bonds and equations. Add equal groups to prepare for multiplication.

### 29. Subtract using the Make 20 Strategy
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-10 ○ 1-60
- **Description:** Lay the foundation for single-digit subtraction. Subtract to "Make 20," then take away the rest. What's 23-5? Visualize 23 on ten frames – 2 tens and 3 more. Imagine taking away 3 counters to make 20, then taking away 2 more. What's left? A full ten frame and 8 more. Verify 23-5 = 18 by drawing and crossing off counters.

### 30. Subtract using the Make 30 Strategy
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-29 ○ 1-60
- **Description:** Generalize from making 20 to making 30. What's 34-6? Visualize 34 on ten frames. Imagine first taking away 4 to make 30, then taking away 2 more. How many are left? 2 full ten frames and 8 more. Verify 34-6 = 28 by drawing and crossing off counters. Model first with ten frames. When ready, transition to number bonds.

### 31. Subtract using the Make 10s Strategy
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 2-30 ○ 1-60
- **Description:** Generalize from making 30 to making multiples of 10. What's 75-7? Use Base 10 blocks to model 75 as 70 and 5. Take away 5 to make 70, then take away 2 more to make 68. Use Base 10 blocks to model and subtract concretely. Transition to number bonds and mental math to subtract more abstractly.

### 32. Subtract Multiples of 10
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5, 2.NBT.8
- **Prerequisites:** 1-118
- **Description:** Use Base 10 discs to subtract a multiple of 10, and an equation to show explicitly how many tens and ones there are to start, how many are taken away, and how many are left. What's 54-30? Subtracting 3 tens from 5 tens and 4 ones leaves 2 tens and 4 ones, so 54-30 = 24. Transition to number bonds and equations.

### 33. Subtract Double-Digit Numbers with No Regrouping
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 2-32
- **Description:** Subtract 2-digit numbers with no regrouping. What's 59-32? 59 is 50 and 9, or 5 tens and 9 ones. 32 is 30 and 2, or 3 tens and 2 ones. Since 50-30 = 20, 9-2 = 7, and 20+7 = 27, 59-32 = 27. Subtract tens and ones to find the partial differences. Add to get the total difference. Transition to number bonds and equations.

### 34. Subtract Double-Digit Numbers with Regrouping using Base 10 Discs
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 2-33 ○ 1-102
- **Description:** Use Base 10 discs to model subtraction regrouping problems. What's 45–16? 45 is 40 and 5. 16 is 10 and 6. There are not enough ones, so shift ten. "Forty-five" becomes "thirty-fifteen." Since 30-10 = 20, 15-6 = 9, and 20+9 = 29, 45-16 = 29. Find and add the partial differences to get the total difference.

### 35. Subtract Double-Digit Numbers with Regrouping using Number Bonds
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5
- **Prerequisites:** 2-34 ○ 1-103
- **Description:** Transition from using concrete Base 10 discs to model regrouping problems, to using pictorial number bonds. What's 64–28? 64 in expanded form is 60 and 4. 28 is 20 and 8. There are not enough ones, so shift 10. "Sixty-four" becomes "fifty-fourteen." Since 50-20 = 30, 14-8 = 6, and 30+6 = 36, 64-28 = 36.

## 4. Word Problems to 100

### 36. Add-To or Put-Together Result-Unknown Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-13 ○ 2-26, 2-25
- **Description:** Model "add-to" problems where the "result" or total – the sum of 2 parts – is unknown. Use a part-total diagram to show the relationship between the starting amount, the "change" or part being added, and the result. Write and solve an addition equation to find the unknown total.

### 37. Add-To or Put-Together Change-Unknown Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-65 ○ 2-26, 2-25
- **Description:** Model "add-to" problems where the "change" or second addend – one of the parts – is unknown. Use a part-total diagram to show the relationship between the starting amount, the "change" or part being added, and the result or total. Write and solve an addition or subtraction equation to find the unknown part.

### 38. Add-To or Put-Together Start-Unknown Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-66 ○ 2-35, 2-33, 2-26, 2-25
- **Description:** Model challenging, "add-to" problems where the "start" or first addend – one of the parts – is unknown. Use a part-total diagram to show the relationship between the starting amount, the "change" or part being added, and the result or total. Write and solve an addition or subtraction equation to find the unknown start, or part.

### 39. Add-To or Put-Together Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1, 2.NBT.9
- **Prerequisites:** 2-38 ○ 2-35, 2-33, 2-26, 2-25
- **Description:** Model and solve "add-to" problems where the result (easier), change (moderate), or start (harder) is unknown. Add to find an unknown total. Add or subtract to find an unknown part. Use the relationship between operations and properties of operations to make adding and subtracting easier.

### 40. Take-From or Take-Apart Result-Unknown Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-69 ○ 2-35, 2-33, 2-26, 2-25

Use a part-total diagram to show the relationship between the starting total, the "change" or part taken away, and the result – the part that's left. Write and solve a subtraction or addition equation to find the unknown part.

### 41. Take-From or Take-Apart Change-Unknown Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-70 ○ 2-40
- **Description:** Model more challenging "take-from" problems where the "change" or part taken away – is unknown. Use a part-total diagram to show the relationship between the starting total, the "change" or part taken away, and the result – the part that's left. Write and solve a subtraction or addition equation to find the unknown part.

### 42. Take-From or Take-Apart Start-Unknown Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-71 ○ 2-41
- **Description:** Model challenging "take-from" problems where the "start" or total is unknown. Use a part-total diagram to show the relationship between the starting total, the "change" or part taken away, and the result – the part that's left. Write and solve an addition equation to find the unknown start, or total.

### 43. Take-From or Take-Apart Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1, 2.NBT.9
- **Prerequisites:** 2-42 ○ 1-72
- **Description:** Model and solve "take-from" word problems where the result (easier), change (moderate), or start (harder) is unknown. Add to find an unknown total. Subtract or add to find an unknown part. Use the relationship between operations and properties of operations to make subtracting and adding easier.

### 44. Comparison Difference-Unknown Keyword-More Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-73
- **Description:** Model comparison problems in which the keywords "greater" and "more" are used to describe the difference between 2 quantities. Lisa read 52 books. David read 17 books. How many more books did Lisa read than David? Since 52-17 = 35, Lisa read 35 more books than David. Subtract or add to find the difference.

### 45. Comparison Difference-Unknown Keyword Fewer Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-44 ○ 1-73
- **Description:** Transition to comparison problems in which the keywords "less" and "fewer" are used to describe the difference between 2 quantities. Emily read 92 books. Jake read 13 books. How many fewer books did Jake read than Emily? Since 92-13 = 79, Jake read 79 fewer books than Emily. Subtract or add to find the difference.

### 46. Comparison Larger-Unknown Keyword-More Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-74
- **Description:** Model problems where the smaller number and difference are known, and the larger number is unknown. Use the keyword "more" in an intuitive way. Kia has 25 grapes. Ed has 18 more grapes than Kia. How many grapes does Ed have? Since 25+18 = 43, Ed has 43 grapes. Understand context to make sense of keywords.

### 47. Comparison Larger-Unknown Keyword Fewer Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-46
- **Description:** Transition to challenging problems where the smaller number and difference are known, but the words "less" and "fewer" are used to describe the difference. Ed has 47 grapes. Ed has 14 fewer grapes than Kia. How many grapes does Kia have? Ed has fewer, so Kia has more. Since 47+14 = 61, Kia has 61 grapes.

### 48. Comparison Smaller-Unknown Keyword Fewer Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-75
- **Description:** Model problems where the larger number and difference are known, and the smaller number is unknown. Use the keywords "less" and "fewer" in an intuitive way. Kyle picked 63 grapes. Andy picked 18 fewer grapes than Kyle. How many grapes did Andy pick? 63-18 = 45, so Andy picked 45 grapes. Use context to make sense of keywords.

### 49. Comparison Smaller-Unknown Keyword-More Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-48
- **Description:** Transition to challenging problems where the larger number and difference are known, but the word "more" is used to describe the difference. Ed picked 41 grapes. Ed picked 18 more grapes than Kia. How many grapes did Kia pick? Ed picked more, so Kia picked fewer. Since 41-18 = 23, Kia picked 23 grapes.

### 50. Comparison Keyword-More Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-49 ○ 2-46, 2-44.2, 2-26
- **Description:** Model comparison problems in which the keyword "more" is used to describe the difference. Include problems where the difference, the larger number, or the smaller number is unknown. Interpret and understand context to make sense of how "more" is being used.

### 51. Comparison Keyword Fewer Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-48 ○ 2-47, 2-45
- **Description:** Generalize to comparison problems in which the keyword "fewer" is used to describe the difference. Include problems where the difference, the smaller number, or the larger number is unknown. Interpret and understand context to make sense of how "fewer" is being used.

### 52. Comparison Word Problems
- **Standards:** 2.OA.1
- **Prerequisites:** 2-51, 2-50
- **Description:** Assess understanding of additive comparison by solving problems where the keyword "more" or "fewer" is used to describe the difference. Include problems where the difference, larger number, or smaller number is unknown. Interpret and understand context to make sense of how keywords are being used.

## 5. Fraction Foundations

### 53. Model Unit Fractions using Linear Bar Models
- **Standards:** 2.G.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-82
- **Description:** Use linear bar models to model 1 half, 1 third, 1 fourth, and 1 eighth. Draw halves by dividing the whole – the length of a bar – into 2 equal parts. Make thirds by drawing 3 equal parts. Make fourths or quarters by dividing halves in half to make 4 equal parts. Make eighths by dividing fourths in half to make 8 equal parts.

### 54. Compare Unit Fractions using Rods & Bar Models
- **Standards:** 2.G.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 1-85 ○ 2-53
- **Description:** Use colored rods and bar models to compare unit fractions. Explore the relationship between the number of equal parts and their size. For the same whole, more equal parts means smaller parts, and fewer equal parts means larger parts. There is an inverse relationship between the number of parts in the whole and their size.

### 55. Model Proper Fractions using Rods & Bar Models
- **Standards:** 2.G.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-53
- **Description:** Use colored rods and draw bar models to represent proper fractions - fractions less than a whole. If the bar is divided into thirds - 1 third is a unit fraction, 2 thirds is a proper fraction, and 3 thirds is the whole. Since a unit is less than the whole, it is also a proper fraction.

### 56. Model Proper Fractions Flexibly
- **Standards:** 2.G.3
- **Notes:** Co-presented
- **Prerequisites:** 2-55
- **Description:** Generalize about equal parts by modeling fractions in different ways. What does 2 thirds mean? 2 out of 3 equal parts - any 2. The go-to way to show proper fractions using a bar model is left-to-right, with no gaps - a length model. Encourage flexibility by modeling them right-to-left, and with gaps - an area model.

### 57. Model Unit & Proper Fractions using Square Area Models
- **Standards:** 2.G.3
- **Prerequisites:** 2-56 ○ 1-83
- **Description:** Generalize from using linear, bar models to model fractions, to using square, area models. Think of area as the space "inside" a square. Draw horizontal and vertical lines to show 2 halves and 3 thirds. Divide halves in half to draw fourths. Divide fourths in half to draw eighths. Transition from unit fractions to proper fractions.

### 58. Partition Circles into Equal Parts
- **Standards:** 2.G.3
- **Prerequisites:** 2-57 ○ 1-84, 1-81
- **Description:** Generalize from partitioning squares to partitioning circles into 2, 3, 4, and 8 equal parts. Start by drawing a dot in the center. Draw a line thru the center to make 2 halves. Draw 3 connecting lines from the center to the circle's edge to make 3 thirds. Divide halves in half to make fourths. Divide fourths in half to make eighths.

### 59. Partition Polygons into Equal Parts*
- **Standards:** 2.G.3
- **Prerequisites:** 2-58
- **Description:** For enrichment, partition equilateral triangles, rotated squares, and regular (equal-sided) octagons into equal parts – 4 triangles that all have the same size and shape. Start by drawing a dot in the shape's center. Make 2, 3, 4, or 8 equal parts by drawing lines to, or thru, the center. Transition from unit fractions to proper fractions.

### 60. Model Fractions with Non-Standard Parts
- **Standards:** 2.G.3

- **Description:** Use a bar model to add using the Make 10s strategy on a number line. What's 18+6? Draw a bar of length 18. 2 more are needed to make 20, so think of 6 as 2 and 4. Since 18+2 = 20 and 20+4 = 24, 18+6 = 24. Use number lines to visualize how much more is needed to make the nearest 10, then add the rest.

### 122. Number Lines - Subtract using the Make 10s Strategy
- **Standards:** 2.NBT.5, 2.MD.6
- **Prerequisites:** 2-120 ○ 2-31
- **Description:** Generalize from adding to subtracting. What's 22-6? Draw a bar of length 22. Need to take away 2 to make 20, so think of 6 as 2 and 4. Since 22-2 = 20 and 20-4 = 16, 22-6 = 16. Use number lines to visualize how much needs to be taken away to make the nearest 10, then subtract the rest.

### 123. Money - Identify & Compare Coins
- **Standards:** 2.MD.8
- **Prerequisites:** K-120, K-119
- **Description:** Identify the names and attributes of pennies, nickels, dimes, quarters, and $1 bills. Learn and compare their value in cents (¢). Compare their size. For quarters, nickels, and pennies, larger size means greater value. Dimes are the exception. They are smaller in size than pennies and nickels, but greater in value.

### 124. Money - Add & Compare Groups of Coins
- **Standards:** 2.MD.8
- **Prerequisites:** 2-123 ○ 1-129
- **Description:** Reinforce the value of pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters by finding the total value of a group with up to 4 coins. Compare groups using the greater than (>), less than (<), or equal (=) symbol.

### 125. Tell Time to the Nearest 15 Minutes
- **Standards:** 2.MD.7
- **Prerequisites:** 1-132
- **Description:** Use an analog clock to model times to the nearest quarter hour. What time is it when the hour hand is between 7 and 8 and the minute hand is pointing at 3? Since there are 60 minutes in 1 hour, 15 minutes is 1/4 of an hour. The time is 7:15 – 7 hours and 15 minutes past 12:00 – or "quarter past 7."

### 126. Tell Time to the Nearest 5 Minutes
- **Standards:** 2.MD.7
- **Prerequisites:** 2-125
- **Description:** Model time to the nearest 5 minutes. What time is it when the hour hand is between 1 and 2 and the minute hand is pointing at 4? Since 12 hours represents a total of 60 minutes, 1 hour represents 5 minutes and 4 hours represents 20 minutes. The time is 1:20 – 1 hour and 20 minutes after 12:00 – or "20 minutes after 1."

### 127. Tell Time: A.M. vs P.M.
- **Standards:** 2.MD.7
- **Prerequisites:** 1-133
- **Description:** Help students better understand time by matching common activities and events, to specific times of the day. Do kids go to school at 8:05 a.m. or 8:05 p.m.? Do they eat an after-school snack at 3:10 a.m. or 3:10 p.m.? Use a.m. to indicate times before noon. Use p.m. to indicate times after noon.

## 11. Data

### 128. Vertical Picture Graphs within 10
- **Standards:** 2.MD.10
- **Prerequisites:** 1-5
- **Description:** Use vertical picture graphs to make sense of data and provide context for operations. Which category has the greatest number of objects – the most? Which has the least number of objects – the fewest? Find the sum of any 2 categories. Compare and quantify their differences. How many objects in all – the sum of 4 addends?

### 129. Vertical Bar Graphs within 10
- **Standards:** 2.MD.10
- **Prerequisites:** 2-128
- **Description:** Begin to connect quantity and length by transitioning from picture graphs to bar graphs. Represent 1 object with 1 unit square or unit bar. Since there are no gaps, the number of objects in each category is represented by the length of each bar and given by the graph's vertical axis. Compare and calculate sums and differences.

### 130. Horizontal Picture Graphs within 20
- **Standards:** 2.MD.10
- **Prerequisites:** 2-128 ○ 1-4
- **Description:** Generalize from vertical to horizontal picture graphs. Use data to provide context for operations, and graphs to represent numbers visually and build number sense. Use grouping strategies based on multiples of 5 and 10 to calculate and compare 1 category, 2 categories, and calculate how many in all.

### 131. Horizontal Bar Graphs within 20
- **Standards:** 2.MD.10
- **Prerequisites:** 2-130 ○ 2-129
- **Description:** Connect quantity and length by transitioning from picture graphs to bar graphs. Represent 1 object with 1 unit bar. Since there are no gaps or overlaps, the number of objects in each category is represented by the length of each bar and given by the graph's horizontal axis. Compare and calculate sums and differences.

### 132. Line Plots with Standard Units
- **Standards:** 2.MD.9
- **Prerequisites:** 2-128
- **Description:** Introduce line plots as a visual way to make sense of data and provide context for measuring length and weight. Plot the frequency of lengths measured to the nearest inch or foot, and the measurement of weight measured to the nearest ounce or pound. What's the minimum length or weight? What's the maximum length or weight? Which length or weight is observed most? Explore data both as individual observations and also as a group.

### 133. Line Plots with Metric Units
- **Standards:** 2.MD.9
- **Prerequisites:** 2-132
- **Description:** Draw line plots to make sense of data measured in centimeters, meters, grams and kilograms. Start by drawing a number line that includes all the values in the sample. Write the smallest and largest values first, followed by all the numbers in-between. Plot the frequency of each length or weight to better understand the sample's data as a group.

### 134. Defining Attributes of 2D Shapes
- **Standards:** 2.G.1
- **Prerequisites:** 1-134
- **Description:** Explore the defining attributes – number of sides, equal sides, vertices, and square corners – that determine if a shape is a triangle, quadrilateral, rectangle, square, pentagon, or hexagon. ## Overview

In second grade, students focus on four critical areas: extending understanding of base-ten notation; building fluency with addition and subtraction; using standard units of measure; and describing and analyzing shapes. They solve one- and two-step part-total and comparison word problems with addition and subtraction within 100, work with equal groups to lay groundwork for multiplication, and reinforce fluency with their facts within 20. Place value extends to hundreds: students understand three-digit numbers as hundreds, tens, and ones; read, write, and compare numbers to 1,000; and use place-value strategies to add and subtract within 1,000 (including problems with regrouping). Measurement and data instruction involves measuring and estimating lengths in standard units (inches, feet, centimeters, meters), relating addition and subtraction to length on a number line, telling and writing time to the nearest five minutes, solving word problems with money, and creating and interpreting picture and bar graphs. Geometry emphasizes recognizing and drawing shapes with specified attributes (e.g., triangles, quadrilaterals), partitioning rectangles into equal shares (halves, thirds, fourths, eighths), and describing the whole as the sum of the parts. Students use these skills to reason about the world around them, developing precision and the ability to model real-world situations mathematically.
